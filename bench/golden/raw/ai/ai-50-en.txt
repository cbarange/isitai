Craft trades are drawing young trainees again, but workshops struggle to keep them. In one rural department, around fifteen ceramicists have set up in five years, often after a career change. Local demand exists, carried by markets and by buyers looking for traceable objects. Income, however, remains irregular, and several practitioners top it up with introductory classes or commissions from restaurants. A potter three years into the trade says she works six days out of seven and has yet to draw a stable salary. Start-up grants cover equipment, rarely the time needed to reach capacity. Chambers of trades nonetheless report rising registration requests in ceramics and cabinetmaking. The risk, they say, is a fast start followed by closure within two years. Passing on existing workshops remains, for its part, largely unresolved.
