The design of transport protocols rests on a set of trade-offs that the technical literature describes at length. Reliability, throughput and latency cannot be optimised simultaneously, which forces explicit choices according to the intended use. Connection-oriented protocols, TCP foremost among them, privilege ordering and data integrity at the cost of signalling overhead. That overhead becomes problematic when network conditions degrade, since congestion control mechanisms then reduce the achievable rate sharply. Connectionless protocols such as UDP shift the responsibility for reliability up to the application layer. This arrangement yields substantial latency gains in contexts where an isolated loss is tolerable. It is further worth noting that signalling itself constitutes an attack surface, which has motivated the gradual deployment of handshake mechanisms resistant to replay. Comparative evaluations published in recent years suggest that the choice of protocol matters less than the quality of its implementation. This observation invites treating stack configuration and tuning as a research object in its own right.
