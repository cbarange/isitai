Craft trades have been the subject of several anthropological studies concerned with the transmission of know-how. These studies converge on one point: competence is not reducible to knowledge of gestures, it includes the ability to read signals from the material. The potter who judges kiln temperature from flame colour draws on perceptual knowledge that is difficult to formalise in writing. This perceptual dimension explains why apprenticeship remains long even where techniques are documented. Institutional training tends to privilege technical norms, whereas the workshop transmits tolerance thresholds derived from experience. It is also observable that the economic recognition of the trade depends on signature and accompanying narrative more than on incorporated labour time. This configuration weakens practitioners who produce a great deal without building a reputation. Labelling schemes aim precisely at making such quality visible, though their effect varies by region. A sociology of craft markets could illuminate these disparities more systematically.
