Mountain trail use is an interesting object of study insofar as it links individual practice with collective management concerns. Research conducted on this terrain indicates that use concentrates on a limited number of routes even though the marked network remains widely available. This concentration is partly explained by the circulation of digital information, which tends to homogenise route choices. A marked seasonality is also observable, with peaks coinciding with school holidays and fair-weather weekends. From an erosion standpoint, the most heavily used trails show measurable soil loss, particularly on cross-slope sections. Visitor channelling measures such as stone steps and planted barriers substantially reduce this phenomenon when properly maintained. It should nonetheless be stressed that measuring use remains methodologically delicate, given the absence of continuous counts across all routes. Available data generally come from point sampling, whose representativeness must be discussed. Ultimately the question is less about restricting access than about directing flows towards routes able to absorb them.
