A blacksmith turns bar stock into tools, hardware and ornamental work by heating steel and shaping it under a hammer. The day usually begins with the forge, where the fire is brought up to temperature and the stock is laid out by size. Steel is worked between roughly nine hundred and twelve hundred degrees, hot enough to move but not so hot that it burns. The smith draws, upsets, bends and punches the metal, returning it to the fire whenever it loses colour. Accuracy comes from reading that colour and from judging temperature by eye rather than by instrument. Hammer control matters more than raw strength, because a light precise blow saves a second heating. Finished pieces are normalised, then quenched and tempered to trade hardness against toughness. The work is physical and often solitary, and the craft survives on repair commissions as much as on new work.
