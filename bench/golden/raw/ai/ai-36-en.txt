The material organisation of the domestic kitchen has been little studied as such, although it conditions a substantial share of everyday labour. Surveys in the sociology of domestic life show that storage arrangements are never neutral and that they encode hierarchies of value among objects. Utensils judged legitimate occupy accessible space, while others are relegated to impractical zones. This distribution produces a feedback effect, since relegated objects are then used less and become easier to discard. A temporal dimension is also observable, arrangements often inheriting an earlier configuration that occupants have never interrogated. Moving house is, in this respect, a privileged moment for observing such arbitrations. Interviews with urban households suggest that the mental load associated with tidying decreases when storage locations are explicitly named and shared. These findings nonetheless rest on small samples and would require confirmation at a larger scale. Ergonomics and sociology would benefit from being brought together more systematically.
