Search for "S3 2027" and five of the first eight results are a car. The rest are
trend pieces about GPUs and vector databases. Nobody is writing about the thing
that actually has a date attached to it: on 12 January 2027, the EU Data Act makes
it illegal to charge an EU customer for the egress involved in leaving.

That is not a forecast. It is a paragraph in a regulation, and it lands harder on
object storage than on any other part of a cloud bill, because object storage is
the part nobody has a complete inventory of.

The short version

12 January 2027 — Article 29 of the EU Data Act (Regulation 2023/2854) takes
  full effect: no switching fees, egress included, when an EU customer moves to
  another provider or back on-premises.
Everyday egress is still billed. The ban covers the export you do to
  leave. Ordinary traffic, cross-region reads and CDN pulls stay chargeable.
The financial lock-in weakens. The technical lock-in does not: someone still
  has to enumerate what is in the buckets, who can read it, and what it costs.
Three of the five items below are things you cannot learn from a bucket
  listing — and that is the actual 2027 problem.

 

What actually happens

The date, precisely

The Data Act entered into force in January 2024 and became applicable on
12 September 2025. Chapter VI (Articles 23 to 31) targets cloud lock-in. Since
that September date, providers can only charge at cost for switching, customer
notice periods are capped at two months, and a 30-day transition window is
mandatory. From 12 January 2027, the switching charge itself is gone.

Read the scope carefully, because most vendor summaries do not: the ban applies to
data egress and administrative penalties incurred when switching or running in
parallel across providers. It does not abolish fixed-term commitments, and it
does not touch the egress you generate in normal operation. AWS had already
dropped exit fees in March 2024, before the rules required it; the regulation
turns a courtesy into an obligation for everyone.

The practical consequence: leaving becomes a technical project with no invoice
attached, instead of a technical project with a penalty attached. That is a much
better problem, and a much more honest one.

The scale you are working at

In December 2025 AWS reported S3 passing 500 trillion objects, and raised the
maximum single object to 50 TB. Both numbers matter for the same reason: at
that size, the debris is not debris. An abandoned multipart upload of a 50 TB
object keeps its parts — billed as storage, invisible in a listing — until someone
aborts it.

This is not an edge case. Large uploads are the ones that get interrupted: a
laptop closes, a CI job is cancelled, a network drops at hour six. The parts stay.

The new client is not a person

S3 stopped being a place where a human browses files. With S3 Vectors generally
available in December 2025 — two billion vectors per index — a bucket is now a
retrieval layer behind a model, written and read by code that has no idea what a
folder is.

That changes what a credential means. A presigned URL handed to an agent is a
capability with a timer, and the timer is the security control. Which is why the
rclone advisory of October 2026 is worth reading twice: a presigned PUT URL could
read any bucket in the account, fixed in 1.75.2. The client was doing what clients
do — trusting the URL it was given.

The exposure is not theoretical

The Cloud Exposure Index counted 484,142 public buckets and 18.05 billion
files in October 2026, 71.9% of them on S3. Tenable's analysis found sensitive
data in 16.7% of AWS buckets it sampled. Very little of that was a deliberate
decision to publish; most of it was a policy nobody re-read after a migration.

Where it bites

Three failures, in the order they will find you.

1. You cannot renegotiate what you cannot enumerate. A migration plan needs
per-bucket size, object count, version count, incomplete uploads, and where the
egress actually goes. The console shows objects. It does not show the uploads that
never became objects, the non-current versions still billed, or which bucket is
responsible for the egress line.

2. You cannot prove who signed what. After an incident, "an agent with the
wrong key" is not an answer. Which key, which session, which endpoint, which
timestamp — the controls that produce those answers (ABAC on object tags, short
presigned lifetimes, session-scoped credentials) are all configuration you have to
write down.

3. "S3-compatible" is a claim, not a specification. The moment leaving becomes
free, the second provider stops being hypothetical. That is when checksum headers
(CRC64NVME is now the SDK default) start failing against older gateways,
path-style addressing reappears, and a provider's "compatible" turns out to mean
"compatible with what our SDK did in 2023".
