Object storage is the quiet backbone of most modern stacks: buckets behind the
uploads, the backups, the data lake, the static site, the logs nobody reads until
something breaks. Yet the tooling and the advice around it are still shaped by a
world where one team managed one bucket in one region on one provider.

That world is gone. A single engineer now holds credentials for AWS S3, Cloudflare
R2, Backblaze B2, MinIO, Garage and a NAS running Ceph — often in the same week.
The questions that come with that are specific and badly served: why a request
that two policies allow still returns  , what actually happens on day
eight of a seven-day presigned URL, whether an interrupted multipart upload is
still being billed. Most pages answering them were written for a scale the reader
does not have, or for a version of the service that no longer exists.

What this blog covers

Five threads, and a tag for each:

Access — policies, roles, session limits, and the difference between what a
  principal can list and what it can read.
Cost — storage classes, request pricing, egress, and the charges that only
  appear on the invoice three weeks later.
Performance — pagination, parallelism, checksums, the difference between a
  slow listing and a slow network.
Operations — the day-two problems: orphaned parts, lifecycle rules, keys
  that rotate and break everything signed with them.
Clients — what to use to look inside a bucket when the console is not the
  right tool.

How each article is made

Every article follows the same discipline, because a technical blog is only worth
reading if you can check it:

Primary sources only. Vendor documentation, the actual error string, the
  pricing page as it reads today — not another blog post summarising a blog post.
Every number is dated and attributed. Prices move; a figure without a date
  is a rumour with a decimal point.
Errors are pasted as they come out. Not paraphrased, not tidied. The exact
  string is what someone will grep for at 2 a.m.
A diagram explains the mechanism, not decorates the page. If a sentence can
  be replaced by a picture of the request flow, it is.
No invented figures. Where a number cannot be sourced, the article says so
  or leaves it out. A missing measurement is better than a plausible one.

What we measure, and why

Since November 2026 this blog records four events: a page view, an article viewed,
an article read to the end, and a click towards the product. That is the whole
list. Autocapture, session recording, page-leave tracking and performance sampling
are switched off on purpose — a blog of text has no use for them, and the point is
to know whether an article helped, not to watch someone read it.

The honest reason for measuring at all is that it changes what gets written. If an
article is opened often and finished rarely, the title promised something the
opening did not deliver. If it is finished and nobody clicks through, the link to
the product sits in the wrong place, or the product page answers a different
question. Both are fixable, but only if someone looks.

What this is not

It is not a newsletter, and nothing here sits behind an email form. It is not
vendor content either: we build S3 Explorer, a
desktop client for S3-compatible storage, and every article that mentions it says
so at the bottom. The product earns its place by being useful inside the article,
not by being advertised around it.
