In short: from 12 January 2027, Article 29 of the EU Data Act forbids cloud
providers from charging EU customers for the egress involved in leaving. Everyday
egress stays billable. What disappears is the financial penalty — what remains is
the work: knowing what your buckets contain, who can read them, and what they
cost per prefix. Three commands cover most of it, and a bucket browser that shows
them covers the rest.

What actually changes

The regulation (2023/2854) has applied since 12 September 2025. Since that date,
switching charges are capped at the provider's real cost, notice periods cannot
exceed two months, and a 30-day transition window is mandatory. On 12 January
2027 the switching charge drops to zero.

Read the scope before planning a migration budget around it:

Covered: exporting your data and metadata when you switch provider, or when
  you run in parallel across providers.
Not covered: ordinary internet egress, cross-region transfers, CDN pulls and
  daily traffic. Those stay on the invoice.
Not covered either: fixed-term commitments. Reserved capacity and savings
  plans survive the law; proportionate early-termination penalties remain lawful.

So the deadline removes a bill, not a project. The project is inventory.

The three inventories

1. What you are paying for and cannot see. Interrupted multipart uploads keep
their parts. They are billed as storage and absent from every object listing,
which is how a bucket looks empty and bills like it is full. This is the single
most common finding on an inherited account.

2. What exists more than once. Non-current object versions are billable and
invisible in the default view. On a bucket with versioning enabled by policy and
never expired, this is often the largest line of the bill.

3. Who may read what. Tag-based policies let you express tenancy without
enumerating prefixes — but tags do not appear in  , and

Then:

1. Abort what is orphaned, and add an  
   lifecycle rule so it does not come back. The rule does not clean the past; it
   only stops the future.
2. Expire non-current versions with a deliberate retention window, not a
   guess.
3. Tag your buckets for cost allocation, so egress can be attributed before
   anyone asks you to.
4. Test a second provider while nothing forces you to. One bucket, one key,
   one file with a checksum. That twenty minutes replaces a quarter of
   architecture review — and it is the only way to learn whether "S3-compatible"
   means compatible with your client or with someone's 2023 SDK.

Where S3 Explorer helps

The gap the deadline exposes is not storage — it is visibility. A viewer that
lists objects and stops there leaves you exactly where the regulation found you.
