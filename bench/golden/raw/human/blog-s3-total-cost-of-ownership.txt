Every "S3 pricing comparison" ranks providers by the storage line: $0.023 here, $0.00695 there. That number is almost meaningless.

On a real invoice, storage is often a minority. Egress, request volume, retrieval fees, replication and minimum-storage durations decide the bill — and they scale differently for every workload. A provider that is 3× cheaper per stored gigabyte can be 5× more expensive the moment you actually serve the data.

So we modelled it. Eight providers, four workloads, one formula, every component included.

What we modelled (and what we did not)

Monthly cost = storage + egress + requests + base fee, for a steady-state month.

Storage: GB-month, binary gigabytes (1 TB = 1,024 GB), as every provider bills it.
Egress: internet data transfer out, after each provider's free allowance and fair-use rules.
Requests: PUT/LIST and GET class pricing, at the volume each scenario implies.
Not modelled: cross-region replication transfer (treated separately in scenario 4), retrieval fees for cold tiers, Transfer Acceleration, and support plans.

Prices are list prices captured on 30 October 2026 from each vendor's pricing page, in USD. They change constantly — treat the shape of the curves as the durable insight, not the exact dollars.

The five components nobody quotes

1. Storage per GB-month. The headline, and the least interesting.
2. Egress per GB. Ranges from $0 to $0.12 — a 12× spread on the same gigabyte.
3. Requests. Negligible below ~50M/month, decisive above ~500M/month.
4. Minimum durations. Wasabi bills 90 days whether you keep the object 90 days or 90 minutes. GCS Nearline is 30 days, Coldline 90, Archive 365.
5. Minimum billable size. S3 Standard-IA bills 128 KB minimum per object — a million 4 KB objects cost you 32× their real size.

Items 4 and 5 are where "cheap" providers quietly become expensive.

Replication doubles storage and adds inter-region transfer. On AWS, replicating 10 TB across regions costs $0.02/GB in transfer alone — $205 for a one-off, plus double storage forever. B2 and Wasabi stay cheapest because their storage rate is simply lower.

The three traps that break the model

1. Fair-use egress. "Free egress" is not always free. Wasabi ties it to your stored volume; Backblaze caps it at 3× stored; DigitalOcean gives 1 TiB then bills. Only R2 and OVHcloud are unconditional.

2. Minimum storage duration. Wasabi's 90-day minimum means deleting a 10 TB dataset after a week still bills three months — $240 instead of $18. GCS Nearline (30 days) and Archive (365 days) have the same trap. For ephemeral data (CI artifacts, temporary exports), a "cheap" tier can cost more than S3 Standard.

3. Retrieval and request fees at scale. GCS and S3 charge per GB retrieved from cold tiers. At 500M GETs/month, AWS's $0.0004/1,000 becomes $200 — more than the storage of most small buckets.

What the numbers actually say

Backblaze B2 is the cheapest managed provider for low-to-moderate egress. $6.95/TB and a 3× egress allowance cover most application workloads.
Wasabi is the cheapest per GB with free egress, but the 90-day minimum and fair-use policy make it a fit for stable, long-lived data only.
Cloudflare R2 is not the cheapest — it is the safest bet when egress is unpredictable. No minimum duration, no fair-use clause, no egress fee, and it beats AWS on storage by 35%.
OVHcloud is the dark horse: $0.0157/GB, zero egress, zero API fees, EU sovereign. At high egress it is the cheapest thing on the list.
AWS and GCS win on ecosystem, tiering depth, regions and compliance — never on this bill.
MinIO wins below ~30 TB if you already run hardware and value the operational control. Above that, the ops burden grows faster than the savings.

The rule of thumb: compute your egress-to-storage ratio first. Below 30%, buy the cheapest storage. Above 100%, buy the cheapest egress. Everything else is marketing.
