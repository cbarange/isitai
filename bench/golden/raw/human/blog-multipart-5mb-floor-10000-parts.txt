A 60 GB backup fails with a single  : the request never completes, and minutes later the client reports a dropped connection instead of a reason. Splitting the file into parts is the fix — and the moment multipart's own two walls appear, a 5 MiB minimum per part and a hard count of 10,000 parts. One is checked before the first byte, the other after the last.

The short version

A single upload request cannot carry more than 5 GiB. Anything bigger is split into parts first, by the SDK or by you.
Multipart allows each part between 5 MiB and 5 GiB, at most 10,000 parts per object, and no minimum on the last part.
Part size × 10,000 must cover the object. Multiply the 5 GiB maximum part by the parts ceiling and you get 48.8 TiB, the largest object the protocol can hold.
The 5 MiB floor is enforced at the end: all parts upload with  , then   answers   and the object never exists. Measured below.
The damage is bandwidth and orphaned parts, not lost files: a rejected completion leaves every part behind, billed as storage until an abort removes them.

What actually happens

Amazon S3 documents a single   as carrying "up to 5 GB" — 5,368,709,120 bytes, so GiB in practice — and multipart as the path for anything larger. The limits page adds three numbers: part numbers run from 1 to 10,000 inclusive, part size runs from 5 MiB to 5 GiB, and "there is no minimum size limit on the last part of your multipart upload".

That page gives the maximum object size as 48.8 TiB, while the upload guide promises "up to 50 TB". Both are the same ceiling in different units: 10,000 × 5,368,709,120 bytes is 53,687,091,200,000 bytes, or 48.83 TiB, or 53.69 TB. The maximum object size is not an independent rule. It is the parts ceiling multiplied by the maximum part, which is why raising one without the other changes nothing.

 

The part size is therefore a calculation, not a setting: divide the object size by 10,000 and round up to at least 5 MiB. Below that, the upload runs out of part numbers before it runs out of bytes.

What clients do with it is more interesting than the documentation.   0.19.3 — the layer behind   — ships  , which starts from the configured 8 MiB and doubles the chunk size until the part count fits under 10,000, then clamps the result to the 5 MiB floor and the 5 GiB ceiling. | Object | Minimum part to fit 10,000 | Part size the SDK picks | Parts | Last part |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 60 GiB | 6,442,451 B (6.1 MiB) | 8 MiB | 7,680 | 8 MiB |
| 500 GiB | 53,687,092 B (51.2 MiB) | 64 MiB | 8,000 | 64 MiB |

The doubling matters. A 5 TiB object needs 524 MiB parts; the library ships 1 GiB parts, twice the necessary size. When a part fails, the retry re-sends 1 GiB instead of 524 MiB — at 100 Mbit/s, about 90 seconds, paid again on every retry. For very large objects, pass   explicitly.

Where it bites

I ran four uploads against SeaweedFS 4.50 —  , a container on the same machine at   — with   1.43.125. Every part uploaded with a  . Then:

| Parts sent | Result |
|---|---|
| 1 MiB, 1 MiB, 1 MiB, 1 KiB |   — "Your proposed upload is smaller than the minimum allowed object size." |
| 5 MiB − 1 byte, 1 MiB |   — same message |
| 5 MiB, 5 MiB, 1 KiB | object stored, 10,486,784 bytes, ETag   |
| 1 KiB, single part | object stored, 1,024 bytes, ETag   |

 

The floor is a completion-time check. In the first two rows every   succeeded; the rejection came on  , after the whole payload had crossed the network. A client that retries re-sends the same wrong part size and fails identically. The upload looks healthy until the last call.

It is a floor, exactly. 5,242,880 bytes passes, 5,242,879 does not. Code that derives a part size by integer division gets it wrong on the remainder.

The last part is exempt, and must be. A 10,486,784-byte object finished with a 1,024-byte part. Code that pads the final part to 5 MiB uploads megabytes of nothing and corrupts the object's size.

The count is checked at a different moment, with a different code. Parts 9,999 and 10,000 were accepted; 10,001 answered   — during the upload, not at completion, so a client that treats every 400 the same reports the wrong cause. The wording is not portable: SeaweedFS returns the terse  , moto 5.2.5 returns "Part number must be an integer between 1 and 10000, inclusive". Catch on the code, log the message.

A failed completion leaves the parts behind. Nothing deletes them until you write a lifecycle rule, and they are billed as storage at the object's size. An upload that dies at 90% of 5 TiB leaves about 4.5 TiB of orphaned parts — a line on an invoice rather than in a log. Abort in a   block.

The ETag changes shape. The stored value was  : the MD5 of the concatenated part MD5s with the part count appended. The file's own MD5 is  , so a   comparison against the ETag reports corruption on a perfect upload.

Provider ceilings differ. Cloudflare R2 documents multipart objects to 5 TiB with 10,000 parts and parts of 5 MiB to 5 GiB; AWS documents 48.8 TiB. Same protocol, a factor of ten apart — a pipeline sized against one endpoint hits a   on the other, and the message does not say which limit you met.

Sizing by the last successful upload is how this goes wrong. A 100 GiB job works with 8 MiB parts; point it at 5 TiB and the ceiling appears at part 10,001, hours in. At 8 MiB parts, 10,000 × 8 MiB = 78.1 GiB is the largest object that default ever finishes.

Orphaned parts are what the rest of an S3 bill is made of; the reverse problem, capping a single upload before it starts, is covered in presigned POST size limits.

Doing it right

1. Compute the part size from the object, not from a constant.

 

Ask for 5,000 parts rather than 10,000: it leaves room for a retry without re-planning.

2. Pass it to the client explicitly. The default is not a promise.

 

3. Abort in a  . Parts survive a crashed process for as long as the upload ID does; nothing expires them.

 

4. Prove the limit on the endpoint you deploy against. Run the probe against a test bucket: a 1 MiB part must be rejected, a 5 MiB part accepted. Two calls answer what no documentation can: whether this provider enforces the floor at all.

 

5. Verify what landed with the composite rule. Compare the ETag to the value computed from the part MD5s, never to the MD5 of the file.
