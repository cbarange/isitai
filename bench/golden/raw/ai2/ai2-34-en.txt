The bike had been hanging in the garage for eleven years when Tomas finally took it down. The tyres held air long enough to reveal that the tubes were finished, and the chain had become a single rusted line. He had bought it for a commute he never made, then moved to a flat where the stairs were steep and the hallway narrow. What changed was not his fitness but the office, which moved four kilometres closer and installed a rack in the basement. He replaced the tubes, the chain and the brake pads, spending less than a single month of parking. The first ride took twenty-two minutes and left him breathless on the one hill, and he arrived at work absurdly pleased. Within a week he had found a quieter street that added three hundred metres and removed two dangerous junctions. By the second month he had added mudguards, a rack and a light wired to a dynamo, because the autumn had arrived early. A colleague asked whether the rain did not bother him, and he answered honestly that it did, less than the traffic. The bike hangs nowhere now, because it lives by the door, which is the only place a working bicycle belongs.
