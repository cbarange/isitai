The first week of the new system went exactly as the article promised, and the second week it collapsed. Daniel had built a board with three columns, a morning review and a rule against more than two active tasks. By Thursday of the second week he had eleven items in progress and had stopped opening the board at all. What broke was not the method but the assumption behind it: that his day belonged to him. Half his commitments arrived as requests from a manager who did not share his queue and had no visibility into it. Instead of abandoning the system, he changed its boundary and began sending a short list every Friday afternoon. The list named what he would finish, what he would not, and what needed a decision from someone else. The first two were ignored, the third produced a meeting, and the meeting produced a rule about notice periods. Within a month the number of urgent requests fell, not because people had become kinder but because they could now see the cost. The board survived, though the day never became his, which he eventually accepted as the normal condition.
