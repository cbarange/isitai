A mechanical watch does not measure time, it counts oscillations as evenly as it can. At the centre sits a balance wheel or a pendulum, swinging at a rate set by its mass and by the stiffness of its spring. An escapement keeps that motion alive by giving it a small push at each pass, without which it would simply stop. The same escapement lets a toothed wheel advance by one step, which turns the swinging into something countable. A train of gears then divides that rotation to drive the minute hand and, through a further reduction, the hour hand. The mainspring, wound by hand or by a rotor, stores enough energy for a day or two. Accuracy depends on how stable the oscillator is, and temperature is its main enemy, because heat expands metal and changes spring stiffness. Wrist position matters too, since gravity loads the pivots differently as the watch turns. That is why a good adjustment is made in several positions and never in just one. A service every five to seven years replaces the oils before they turn abrasive.
