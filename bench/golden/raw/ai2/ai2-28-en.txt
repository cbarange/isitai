The advice to change passwords every ninety days is well intentioned and largely harmful. It was designed for a world where a stolen credential could be used quietly for months, and it never accounted for human behaviour. Faced with forced rotation, people increment a number or move a character, producing variants that any cracking tool tries first. The resulting passwords are weaker than the originals while creating a false sense of security. The modern recommendation, uniqueness per service and length over complexity, addresses the actual threat, which is reuse across breaches. Critics reply that users cannot remember dozens of unique passwords, and they are right, which is why managers exist. A password manager concentrates risk, admittedly, so its master secret deserves protection that ordinary passwords do not need. Multi-factor authentication is not a substitute for a good password either, but it does contain the damage when one leaks. The defensible position is that strength should be structural, generated and stored rather than invented and recalled. Policies that ignore this shift protect the appearance of diligence rather than the accounts themselves.
