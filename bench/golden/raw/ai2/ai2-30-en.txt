Productivity advice fails most often because it prescribes behaviours without removing the conditions that made them necessary. Telling someone to focus for four hours does nothing if their day is structured around immediate response. The problem is rarely discipline and usually design, specifically the absence of boundaries that others can see. A shared calendar with blocked time works better than personal resolve, because it changes what colleagues expect rather than what you intend. Sceptics answer that such systems are bureaucratic and that talented people simply deliver, which confuses output with sustainability. The same people usually work long, fragmented hours and attribute the result to intensity rather than to the few genuinely focused blocks inside them. Written commitments are the cheapest coordination technology available, and their absence produces meetings that exist only to re-establish shared context. Limiting work in progress is unpopular because it forces explicit refusal, and refusal is a social act, not an administrative one. Any method that ignores this social dimension will be abandoned in the first busy week. The useful question is therefore not which technique to adopt, but which conversation to have.
