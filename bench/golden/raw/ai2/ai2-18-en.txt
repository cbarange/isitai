A password protects you because it has to be guessed, so everything depends on how many attempts an attacker needs. A phrase of four common words chosen at random resists better than an eight-letter word with one letter swapped for a symbol. The reason is simple: length multiplies the space of possibilities, while decorative substitutions follow patterns that tools already know. The real danger is not guessing on the website, which rate limiting protects, but a stolen database attacked offline. That is why serious services never store the password itself, only a fingerprint computed by a deliberately slow function. A password manager solves the practical problem by generating a different secret for every service. A breach at one site then compromises nothing else, which is the whole point. Two-factor authentication adds a barrier that still holds even when the password is known. Changing passwords on a fixed schedule is now discouraged, because it pushes people towards predictable variations. What matters comes down to three words: long, unique, stored properly.
