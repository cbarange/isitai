Commuting by bicycle is usually dismissed as impractical, and that judgement says more about infrastructure than about bicycles. The distances involved in most urban trips are short, under five kilometres, which is well within the range of an untrained rider. The real obstacle is the perception of danger, and perception follows exposure, so a network that feels safe produces more cyclists almost immediately. Opponents argue that weather makes the practice impossible, yet cities with cold and wet climates show higher modal shares than sunnier ones. The reason is simple: rain is uncomfortable, but traffic is frightening, and only one of those can be fixed by policy. Cargo bikes have quietly removed the last practical objection, since a family can now move children and shopping without a car. The health argument is often overstated as a private benefit when it is really a public one, measured in sick days and hospital admissions. What cycling does not solve is the long trip from a distant suburb, and pretending otherwise alienates the voters whose support is needed. The honest case is not that everyone should cycle, but that the short trip should not require a car. Building for that trip costs far less than building for the alternative.
