Taxation is usually debated as a matter of rates, which is the least interesting part of the design. Two systems with identical headline rates can produce very different outcomes depending on the base, the thresholds and the treatment of capital. A narrow base with high rates punishes the few activities still counted, while a broad base with moderate rates distributes the charge more evenly. Deductions are therefore political instruments disguised as technical details, since every exemption defines what a society chooses to encourage. The claim that lower marginal rates automatically raise revenue is conditional at best, and depends on where the current rate sits relative to the revenue-maximising point. Complexity itself is a cost, because compliance consumes hours that produce nothing, and those hours fall hardest on people who cannot hire advisers. Withholding at source is the single most effective anti-evasion measure ever adopted, and it works because it removes the choice. Credits are preferable to deductions when the goal is to help people on low incomes, since a deduction is worthless to someone who pays no tax. Finally, any reform should be judged on its distributional effect, not on its administrative elegance. A tax system is a set of incentives, and pretending otherwise guarantees unintended consequences.
