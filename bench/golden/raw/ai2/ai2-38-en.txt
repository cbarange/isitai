The email arrived on a Sunday evening and said the account had been accessed from a device in another country. Priya changed her password immediately, then spent an hour changing it everywhere else, because she had used the same one since university. The pattern was not laziness but memory: one strong password repeated was the only system she could hold in her head. On Monday she discovered the attacker had taken no money, only her address book, which was enough to send plausible messages to her contacts. A colleague received one and clicked, and the incident spread quietly through the department for two weeks. The IT team ran a session on credential hygiene, and Priya expected to be bored and was instead annoyed at how obvious the advice was. She installed a manager that afternoon and began the tedious work of replacing sixty passwords with generated ones. The first week felt slower, and she mistyped the master passphrase more than once. By the third week she could not remember the old password at all, which she took as proof that the system was working. The address book attack stopped, not because she had become careful, but because the reused secret no longer existed anywhere.
