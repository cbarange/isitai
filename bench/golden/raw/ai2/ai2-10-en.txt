A working method is a repeatable procedure for converting an intention into a delivered output under bounded attention. Its components are a queue of commitments, an explicit definition of done, and a cadence at which the queue is reviewed. Work in progress must be limited, since throughput falls once concurrent open items exceed the capacity to finish them. Decomposition should stop at units that can be completed within a single uninterrupted block, typically ninety minutes. Interruption cost is nonlinear, because reloading context after a break consumes attention that produces no visible output. Prioritisation by expected value and reversibility outperforms prioritisation by urgency alone, urgency being largely a property of external requests. Feedback loops must be short enough that a wrong assumption is detected before the cost of the error compounds. Written capture externalises memory and makes the state of a commitment inspectable by others, which reduces coordination overhead. Review intervals establish a cadence for pruning stale items and for noticing systemic rather than local failures. A method is validated by its ability to survive a week of abnormal load, not by its elegance in a quiet one.
