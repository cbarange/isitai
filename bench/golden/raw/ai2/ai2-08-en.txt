Password security rests on entropy measured in bits, which grows logarithmically with the size of the space from which a secret is drawn. A password chosen by a human from natural language has far less effective entropy than its length suggests, because the search space is constrained by corpus statistics. Hashing must use a slow, salted, memory-hard function such as Argon2id, bcrypt or scrypt, never a general-purpose digest. A unique salt per record defeats precomputed tables and identical-password correlation across accounts. A server-side pepper, stored outside the database, adds a second independent secret whose compromise requires a separate breach. Rate limiting and progressive lockout reduce online guessing but do nothing against offline attack on a stolen hash dump. Multi-factor authentication changes the threat model by requiring a factor that cannot be replayed from the server side. Credential stuffing exploits reuse rather than weakness, which makes uniqueness per service the highest-value property. Rotation policies based on fixed intervals are counterproductive because they encourage predictable structural mutations. Storage in a password manager with a strong master passphrase and an encrypted vault achieves both uniqueness and sufficient entropy.
