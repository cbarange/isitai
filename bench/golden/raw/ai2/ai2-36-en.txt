The watch had stopped sometime in the night, and my grandfather noticed before he had finished his coffee. He did not say anything, just held it to his ear, then put it down with the careful disappointment of a man who has seen this before. It had belonged to his father, who bought it in a port town with money he had not told anyone about. For forty years it had been serviced by the same watchmaker, who retired the previous spring and sold his tools. We found another workshop across the city, run by a woman who trained in Besançon and who looked at the movement for a long time before speaking. She said the oil had dried and the pivots were worn, and that the repair would cost more than the watch was worth as an object. My grandfather nodded and asked her to do it anyway, and she wrote the price on a card without arguing. Three weeks later he wound it at breakfast and set the time from the radio, as he had always done. It ran four seconds fast per day afterwards, which he considered an improvement on the alternative.
