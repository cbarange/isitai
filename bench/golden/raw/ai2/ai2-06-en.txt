A mechanical clock is an oscillator plus an escapement plus a gear train, and its accuracy follows from the stability of the first of these. The balance wheel and hairspring form a resonant system whose period depends on inertia and on spring stiffness, both temperature sensitive. The escapement delivers impulses that sustain the oscillation while simultaneously counting it, which couples timekeeping to friction. The going train divides the escape wheel rate down to the hour and minute hands through wheels and pinions with fixed tooth counts. The motion work, a twelve-to-one reduction, drives the minute hand from the hour wheel and introduces the only deliberate slip in the train. Jewelled bearings reduce pivot friction at the most heavily loaded points, and their condition dominates long-term rate drift. The barrel stores energy in the mainspring, whose torque curve is flattened by a slipping bridle or by a fusee in older designs. Regulation is achieved by altering the effective length or the active coils of the hairspring at the index. Positional variation arises because gravity changes the load on the pivots, so a movement is rated in several positions rather than in one. Servicing intervals depend on lubricant degradation, typically five to seven years for a modern synthetic oil.
