The rapid spread of social networks has transformed the way people communicate. Platforms such as Instagram, TikTok, Snapchat and Facebook promise to connect individuals across distance and time. However, many observers claim that these sites actually make users feel more isolated. In my opinion, social networks do not necessarily make us lonelier by themselves; instead, they can increase loneliness when they are used passively, excessively or as a substitute for real human contact, while remaining useful when they support genuine relationships.

On the one hand, there are strong reasons to think social networks may deepen loneliness. First, they encourage social comparison. Users often present carefully edited versions of their lives, showing holidays, achievements and attractive friends. When people scroll through these highlights, they may feel that everyone else is happier and more socially accepted than they are. This feeling can be especially harmful for teenagers, who are already sensitive to peer approval. Psychological research also indicates that passive consumption of social media, such as viewing many profiles without interacting, is more often linked with lower mood and feelings of exclusion than active use. Second, online interaction can replace face-to-face contact without providing the same emotional warmth. A message may be convenient, but it does not always offer eye contact, physical presence or spontaneous conversation. As a result, individuals may have hundreds of “friends” yet still lack someone to call in a crisis. Third, algorithms often reward attention rather than well-being. Endless feeds and notifications can make users feel busy without feeling connected.

On the other hand, social networks can also reduce loneliness. They help families and friends stay in touch despite distance. For people who live abroad, a video call or a shared photo can maintain bonds that might otherwise fade. Social platforms are also valuable for people with disabilities, illness or social anxiety, who may find it easier to express themselves online than in person. During emergencies or moments of personal difficulty, online support groups can provide comfort to people who might otherwise suffer in silence. In addition, communities based on shared interests, identities or experiences can give members a sense of belonging. Someone who feels misunderstood in their immediate environment may find support, advice and friendship through an online group. These connections are not always superficial; for many users, they are an important part of daily life.

Therefore, the key issue is not whether social networks exist, but how they are used. When users compare themselves to others, scroll for hours or prefer virtual likes to real conversations, loneliness may grow. However, when they use platforms to arrange meet-ups, share meaningful moments or maintain close relationships, social networks can strengthen emotional ties. The problem also arises when online communication becomes the only form of interaction. Humans need physical presence, trust and shared experiences, and these cannot be fully simulated. A balanced approach is possible: students should learn digital skills, set limits on screen time and use social media as a tool to connect, not as an escape from isolation. Parents and teachers should not simply forbid these platforms, but teach users to recognise the difference between digital attention and real intimacy.

In conclusion, social networks do not automatically make us lonelier. They are neutral tools whose effects depend on the way we use them and the quality of the relationships they support. Yet they can worsen loneliness when they encourage comparison, replace face-to-face contact and create the illusion of connection. For this reason, schools, families and governments should help young people use social media critically and responsibly. If online networks become a bridge to real communication rather than a substitute for it, they will help people feel less lonely instead of more isolated.
