Background: Early diagnosis through medical imaging can materially improve survival, reduce treatment burden, and enable disease modification before irreversible tissue damage occurs. However, conventional image interpretation often depends on subtle visual cues, variable reader expertise, and fragmented clinical data, which can delay recognition of high-risk conditions such as pulmonary nodules, early-stage malignancies, cardiovascular disease, and neurodegeneration. The objective of this study was to determine whether a multimodal artificial intelligence framework integrating quantitative imaging features, longitudinal change patterns, and structured clinical variables could improve the early identification of clinically significant disease in a heterogeneous patient population and reduce reliance on subjective visual detection alone.

Methods: We conducted a retrospective, multicenter diagnostic accuracy study of 1,842 adult patients referred for computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, or ultrasound between January 2019 and December 2022. Eligible participants had available baseline imaging, at least one follow-up study within 24 months, and adjudicated clinical outcomes. Reference standards included histopathology, guideline-based clinical diagnosis, major adverse events, or composite progression measures. Preprocessing included resampling, intensity normalization, artifact detection, and quality control. Radiomic features were extracted from regions of interest using standardized segmentation pipelines. A gradient-boosting ensemble and convolutional neural network were trained separately on imaging-derived features and whole-slice embeddings, then fused with age, sex, body mass index, symptom duration, laboratory markers, and prior disease history. Each model was trained using stratified sampling to preserve site, modality, and outcome distribution. Model performance was assessed with area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, calibration, and decision-curve analysis. Internal validation used ten-fold cross-validation; external validation was performed in 368 patients from two independent sites. Inter-reader agreement among three board-certified radiologists served as the comparator.

Results: The integrated model achieved an internal validation area under the curve of 0.91 (95% confidence interval, 0.89–0.93), compared with 0.78 (0.75–0.82) for pooled radiologist consensus in low-suspicion cases and 0.83 (0.80–0.86) for clinical variables alone. At a fixed sensitivity of 90%, specificity increased from 49% to 67%. The fused model outperformed imaging-only and clinical-only architectures in all validation cohorts, with the largest gains for low-prevalence outcomes and ambiguous findings. In the external cohort, discrimination remained stable (area under the curve, 0.87; 0.84–0.90), with sensitivity of 88% and specificity of 64% at the operating threshold optimized for screening-like decision-making. Subgroup analyses showed the largest benefit for lesions smaller than 8 mm, early fibrotic or vascular changes, and patients with incomplete symptom profiles. In sensitivity analyses that excluded studies with severe motion artifact, poor contrast enhancement, or incomplete follow-up, the performance decline was less than 0.02 in area under the curve, supporting robustness under imperfect data conditions. The model reduced estimated missed diagnoses by 22% over 12 months and increased median time to diagnostic confirmation by 37 days for high-risk individuals without established disease. Calibration slopes ranged from 0.94 to 1.02, and decision-curve analysis demonstrated net benefit across threshold probabilities from 8% to 26%.

Conclusion: Early diagnosis through medical imaging can be enhanced by combining quantitative image phenotyping with longitudinal and clinical context using machine learning. The integrated approach improved discrimination and case-finding efficiency, especially for subtle or preclinical abnormalities, while maintaining acceptable calibration and net clinical benefit in an external cohort. These findings support prospective evaluation of imaging-based decision-support tools that prioritize high-risk patients for confirmatory testing, shorten diagnostic uncertainty, and enable earlier therapeutic intervention. If validated prospectively, such tools may support triage, reduce diagnostic delays, and enable earlier, less intensive interventions. Implementation should include clinician oversight, site-specific threshold optimization, prospective impact assessment, workflow integration, and safeguards against automation bias and equity gaps arising from population variation in imaging quality, disease prevalence, and access to follow-up care.
