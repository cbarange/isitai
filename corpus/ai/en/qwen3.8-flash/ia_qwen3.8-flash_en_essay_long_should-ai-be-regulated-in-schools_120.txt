Artificial intelligence has moved from science fiction to ordinary classroom reality. Students can now use chatbots to summarize books, solve math problems, generate essays, or learn languages, while teachers use AI to design exercises and grade drafts. This situation raises a difficult question: should AI be regulated in schools? I believe it should, but regulation must not mean blanket prohibition. Schools need clear, ethical, and practical rules that protect learning while allowing students to benefit from new technology.

First, AI must be regulated because it can undermine the purpose of education. The goal of school is not only to produce correct answers, but to teach students how to think, research, draft, revise, and reflect. If a learner can ask an AI to write an entire essay in seconds, the temptation to cheat becomes strong. Without rules, students may hand in work that is not their own and receive grades they have not earned. This is unfair to honest students and useless for the learner, because writing and reasoning are essential skills. A simple school policy could state when AI may be used, when it may not, and how students must declare AI assistance. Such rules preserve academic integrity.

Second, AI tools collect personal data and can pose privacy risks. Many applications are not designed specifically for children, and they may store prompts, names, or information about students’ difficulties. Schools have a duty to protect minors and their private information. Regulation could require approved platforms, parental consent where needed, and transparent data policies. In this way, AI can be used in a safe environment. Without control, schools might expose students to commercial tracking, inappropriate content, or unclear conditions of use. Therefore, regulation is not an obstacle to innovation; it is a way to make innovation responsible.

Third, AI can widen inequalities if access is uneven and uncontrolled. Some students have expensive subscriptions or strong digital support at home, while others do not. If schools simply ignore AI, privileged pupils may use it as a powerful private tutor, while disadvantaged students may either misuse it or miss its benefits. Regulation can help reduce this gap by providing equal access through school devices, teaching digital skills to all, and setting common expectations. Moreover, AI systems can contain biases because they learn from human data. Without critical education, students may accept AI outputs as neutral truths. Schools should regulate not only technology, but also teach students to question sources, compare answers, and recognize errors.

However, regulation should not become excessive prohibition. AI is already present in higher education and many professions. If schools ban it completely, students may leave education without understanding how to use it wisely. The real danger is not technology itself, but unprepared use. A total ban could also be impossible to enforce, pushing AI use into hidden areas where no educational support exists. Therefore, a balanced policy is necessary. Schools can allow AI for brainstorming, language practice, or feedback, but not for final production unless the task specifically evaluates AI skills. This approach prepares students for the future while protecting learning.

Finally, regulation should include teachers, parents, and students. Rules created without them may be unrealistic. Teachers need training to detect misuse, design meaningful assessments, and explain ethical limits. Parents should understand school policies and encourage responsible use at home. Students themselves must participate in creating classroom agreements, because they know how AI is actually used. Regulation is not only a list of punishments; it is a shared framework that gives students the right tools. It should emphasize responsibility, honesty, respect, and critical thinking rather than fear.

In conclusion, AI should be regulated in schools because it affects learning, privacy, equality, and academic integrity. Yet regulation must be flexible, educational, and fair. Schools should neither ignore AI nor forbid it blindly. Instead, they should develop clear rules, train staff, protect data, and teach students to use AI as a tool, not as a replacement for thinking. If managed well, AI can enrich education; if left unregulated, it may weaken it. The aim is not to stop progress, but to guide it so that technology serves human learning rather than the other way around.
