The immune system is the set of biological structures and processes that protects organisms from pathogens, damaged cells, and foreign substances. It includes organs such as bone marrow, thymus, lymph nodes, and spleen, and is commonly divided into innate and adaptive components. Innate immunity provides immediate defense through physical barriers such as skin and mucous membranes, as well as through cells and molecules that recognize broad patterns associated with microbes. These include phagocytes, natural killer cells, complement proteins, and inflammatory signaling pathways. Adaptive immunity develops more slowly but creates specificity and memory. It relies mainly on lymphocytes, especially B cells and T cells, which respond to particular antigens. B cells produce antibodies, while T cells coordinate immune responses or destroy infected cells directly. This division helps organisms respond efficiently to diverse threats while limiting damage to normal tissues. Immune function is influenced by age, genetics, nutrition, stress, and prior exposure to pathogens or vaccines. When immune regulation fails, the system may become underactive, leading to increased susceptibility to infection, or overactive, contributing to allergy, autoimmunity, or chronic inflammation. Medical interventions, including antibiotics, immunization, and immune-modulating therapies, depend on understanding these mechanisms.
