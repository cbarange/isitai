At first the word meant nothing to Alma—demolition, a heavy word her father set down between the salt and the bread as though it were another dish no one wanted. She was nine, and words at supper usually belonged to grown-ups: invoices, weather, the neighbor’s dog. But her mother’s fork stopped halfway to her mouth, and the kitchen went so quiet that Alma could hear the refrigerator shudder and hush.

“When?” her mother asked.

“They signed the papers today,” her father said. “The road goes through in the spring. We have until March.”

Alma looked at the wall behind him, at the pencil marks climbing the doorframe like a small, private ladder. Her name was at the bottom in her mother’s careful hand, then again higher, then higher, each date a rung she had climbed without noticing. Demolition, she thought. She pictured a machine with iron teeth. She pictured the house folding into itself like a paper box.

“What does it mean?” she asked.

Her father rubbed his thumb along the rim of his glass. “It means the house will be taken down,” he said. “The city’s taking the land.”

“Taken down,” Alma repeated. She imagined the roof lifted off first, then the rooms left open to the sky, the wallpaper exposed to rain. The thought made her stomach feel hollow. She had lived in this house all her life. She knew which stair groaned, which window stuck in summer, where the floorboards dipped near the stove. She knew the house the way she knew her own hands.

After supper she went outside. The evening was blue and ordinary. Mrs. Bell’s curtains glowed across the street; a bicycle leaned against the fence. Alma walked around the side of the house and pressed her palm to the brick. It was warm from the day. In the narrow bed by the porch, her mother’s roses had gone leggy and dark. Alma had never thought of the house as something that could be decided upon, signed away, reduced to a line on a map. It had always simply been there, holding their coats and their birthdays, their burned dinners and their arguments, the black scuff on the hall wall where she had ridden her tricycle indoors.

She went back inside and up to her room. The ceiling sloped over her bed. She lay down and looked at the crack in the plaster that branched like a river. Once, she had been afraid it would split open in the night. Now she felt sorry for it. She touched the wall and whispered, “I’m sorry.” Then she felt foolish and turned her face into the pillow.

Later, her mother came to sit on the edge of the bed. She did not turn on the light. “It’s all right to be sad,” she said. “I’m sad too.”

“Will they take the door with the marks?”

Her mother was quiet. “No, love.”

Alma nodded in the dark. She thought of all the things the house would not carry with it: the smell of onions in the hallway, the squeak of the third stair, the square of sun that crossed the kitchen floor every morning and made a warm place for the cat. She thought of her own voice caught in the corners. It seemed to her that a house must keep some of the people who lived in it, the way a coat keeps the shape of a shoulder.

“Where will we go?” she asked.

“Somewhere new,” her mother said. “We’ll make it ours.”

Alma closed her eyes. Outside, a car passed, and its headlights slid across the sloped ceiling, room after room, as if searching the house for something it had lost. She listened to the stair groan under her father’s foot, to the refrigerator shudder and hush. In the morning, she decided, she would walk through every room and say goodbye quietly, so the house would know it had been loved before it was taken down.
