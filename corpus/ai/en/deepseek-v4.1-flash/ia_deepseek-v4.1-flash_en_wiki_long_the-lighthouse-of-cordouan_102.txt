The Cordouan Lighthouse, known in French as the Phare de Cordouan, stands on a rocky limestone plateau in the Gironde estuary near the mouth of the Garonne and Dordogne rivers on France’s Atlantic coast. It is located in the department of Gironde, approximately seven kilometres offshore from Royan and within the commune of Le Verdon-sur-Mer; access is normally by boat from Royan or Le Verdon, subject to tides and weather. UNESCO inscribed the lighthouse on the World Heritage List in 2021, citing it as a masterpiece of maritime signalling and an outstanding example of lighthouse architecture. The French Ministry of Culture records it as a monument historique, a status granted in 1862. It is commonly described as the oldest lighthouse in France still in service.

A navigational light at Cordouan existed in the Middle Ages. Historical accounts attribute the first tower to Edward the Black Prince, Prince of Wales, who ordered its construction in the 1360s to guide shipping through the hazardous estuary. That medieval structure was a square tower topped by a fire, but it deteriorated and was damaged over the following two centuries. By the late sixteenth century, the need for a more durable and visible beacon prompted a major rebuilding campaign.

The present lighthouse was built between 1584 and 1611 under the patronage of Henry III and Henry IV, with the architect Louis de Foix responsible for its design. It is noted for its Renaissance architecture, which is unusual for a functional maritime structure. Its lower levels incorporated ceremonial and residential spaces, including a royal apartment and a chapel, reflecting the lighthouse’s symbolic as well as practical role. The original tower was lower than the present structure and was subsequently modified and heightened.

In the late eighteenth century, the engineer Joseph Teulère directed works between 1786 and 1790 that raised the tower and altered its profile, giving it much of its current appearance. The lighthouse now reaches a height of approximately 67.5 metres, with about 300 steps leading to the lantern. Its light, modernised repeatedly, remains an active aid to navigation at the mouth of the Gironde, one of the more dangerous sections of the French Atlantic coast. In 1823 Cordouan was among the first lighthouses to be equipped with a Fresnel lens, an innovation that greatly improved the efficiency of lighthouse optics. Further upgrades in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries kept the station in line with developments in navigational technology.

UNESCO’s 2021 inscription recognises Cordouan as an exceptional testimony to the history of maritime safety and lighthouse engineering. The nomination cited the lighthouse’s architectural quality, its long continuity of use and its role in the development of navigational aids. The property illustrates the relationship between monumental architecture and coastal navigation, combining a Renaissance tower with later technical additions. Conservation is overseen by French heritage authorities, and the lighthouse is maintained as both a working navigational facility and a heritage monument.

The Cordouan Lighthouse remains a landmark of the Gironde estuary and a symbol of France’s maritime heritage. Its survival reflects successive phases of construction, adaptation and restoration, from a medieval beacon to a Renaissance tower and then to a modern navigational station. It is accessible to visitors under regulated conditions, subject to weather and tidal constraints, and continues to be studied by historians and engineers interested in the development of lighthouse design.
