Plate tectonics is the scientific theory that describes the large-scale motion of Earth’s lithosphere, the rigid outer shell that includes the crust and uppermost mantle. According to this framework, the lithosphere is divided into a mosaic of tectonic plates that move relative to one another over the weaker, partially molten asthenosphere. The theory, developed in its modern form during the 1960s, integrated earlier ideas of continental drift and seafloor spreading and became the unifying paradigm of the geosciences because it explains the distribution of earthquakes, volcanoes, mountain belts, ocean basins, and many long-term geological and biological patterns (Wegener, 1912; Hess, 1962; Wilson, 1965).

The historical development of plate tectonics involved several stages. In the early twentieth century, Alfred Wegener proposed continental drift, arguing from the fit of continental margins, fossil correlations, and paleoclimatic evidence that continents had once formed larger landmasses and later separated. His hypothesis was widely debated because it lacked a plausible mechanism. During the 1930s and 1940s, Arthur Holmes and others suggested that convection currents in the mantle could provide the driving force. In the 1950s and 1960s, marine geophysical surveys revealed the global mid-ocean ridge system, and Harry Hess proposed seafloor spreading: new oceanic lithosphere forms at ridges and moves away as the ocean basins widen. Magnetic stripe patterns on the seafloor, interpreted by Vine and Matthews (1963) as records of geomagnetic reversals, provided decisive support by showing symmetrical anomalies about mid-ocean ridges. Earthquake distribution and focal mechanisms further defined the boundaries of rigid plates (Isacks, Oliver, and Sykes, 1968).

Earth’s lithosphere is commonly divided into major plates—such as the Pacific, North American, Eurasian, African, Antarctic, Indo-Australian, and South American plates—and numerous smaller plates. These plates move at rates typically ranging from about one to ten centimeters per year, with some local variations, as measured by space geodesy and magnetic anomaly dating. Their boundaries are classified as divergent, convergent, or transform. At divergent boundaries, plates move apart and new lithosphere is created, as along mid-ocean ridges and in continental rift zones such as the East African Rift. At convergent boundaries, one plate typically subducts beneath another where at least one is oceanic, producing deep-sea trenches, volcanic arcs, and earthquakes; where continents collide, crustal thickening builds mountain ranges such as the Himalayas. At transform boundaries, plates slide past one another, as along the San Andreas Fault, generating shallow earthquakes.

The driving forces of plate motion are still an active area of research, but the main contributions are generally attributed to slab pull, ridge push, and mantle convection. Slab pull, the negative buoyancy of cold, dense subducting lithosphere, is widely regarded as the dominant force, while ridge push arises from gravitational sliding away from elevated mid-ocean ridges. Mantle convection provides the broader thermal and mechanical context, although plates are not simply passive rafts on convection cells; their strength, thickness, and boundary interactions influence how they move. Forces resisting motion include basal drag, friction at plate boundaries, and the strength of the lithosphere itself. Finite-element and global circulation models are used to test these force balances against geological and geodetic observations (Conrad and Lithgow-Bertelloni, 2002).

Plate tectonics has wide explanatory power. It accounts for the global distribution of seismicity along narrow belts and the occurrence of deep earthquakes only in subduction zones. It explains the formation of island arcs, continental volcanic arcs, back-arc basins, and many ore deposits associated with magmatic and hydrothermal activity. It provides a framework for understanding the growth and breakup of supercontinents through the Wilson cycle, the opening and closing of ocean basins, and the accretion of terranes at continental margins. It also helps explain long-term climate change through variations in volcanic outgassing, weathering, and ocean circulation, as well as biogeographic patterns produced by the shifting connections and barriers between landmasses. Paleomagnetic data, seafloor age maps, seismic tomography, and GPS measurements are among the primary sources of evidence used to reconstruct plate motions and test tectonic models (DeMets et al., 1990; Müller et al., 2008).

Important questions remain. Researchers continue to investigate how subduction initiates, why plate motions change over time, how plate boundaries evolve and reorganize, and how tectonics interacts with mantle convection, climate, and the biosphere. Comparative studies of other rocky planets and moons also inform understanding of the conditions required for plate tectonics, because Earth is currently the only known body with a confirmed global system of rigid, mobile plates. Overall, plate tectonics remains a robust and extensively supported theory that provides the organizing framework for interpreting Earth’s geological history and present-day dynamics.
