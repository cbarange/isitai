Begin by feeding your sourdough starter four to six hours before mixing so it reaches peak activity. Remove all but a small spoonful from your jar, add equal parts flour and water by weight, and let it sit in a warm place until doubled, domed, and covered in fine bubbles.

For the dough, weigh out 500 grams of bread flour into a large bowl. Pour in 350 grams of room-temperature water, reserving about 50 grams for later. Mix with a sturdy spatula or your hand until the flour is fully hydrated, then cover the bowl with a damp towel or plastic wrap and let it autolyse for one hour.

After the autolyse, add 100 grams of active starter and 10 grams of fine sea salt. Squeeze and massage the mixture through your fingers until the starter and salt are completely incorporated. The dough will be sticky and rough at this stage, which is normal. Avoid the temptation to add extra flour; slack dough produces an open crumb.

Over the next two hours, perform four sets of stretch and folds spaced thirty minutes apart. Wet your hand to prevent sticking, reach under the front of the dough, pull it up until it resists, and fold it over the top. Rotate the bowl a quarter turn and repeat the motion three more times so the entire mass is tightened. After the final set, the dough should feel noticeably stronger and smoother.

Allow the dough to complete bulk fermentation over the following two to three hours. At a room temperature of roughly 72 to 75 degrees Fahrenheit, the total bulk time should be four to five hours. Look for a fifty to seventy-five percent rise in volume, a rounded domed surface, and active bubbles pushing against the sides of the bowl.

Gently tip the dough onto a lightly floured work surface. Shape it into a loose round by pulling the outer edges toward the center, flipping it over so the seams face down, and letting tension build. Cover it with an inverted bowl and let the pre-shape rest for twenty to thirty minutes until it feels relaxed and slightly puffy.

Turn the dough over so the seam faces up, then perform the final shape. Fold it into a tight boule or oblong batard, pinching the seam firmly closed. Dust a banneton or a bowl lined with a smooth kitchen towel generously with rice flour or coarse semolina. Place the dough seam-side up, cover it lightly, and refrigerate for eight to twelve hours or overnight. The long cold retard develops tangy flavor and firms the surface for clean scoring.

At least one hour before baking, set a heavy Dutch oven with its lid inside your oven and preheat to 500 degrees Fahrenheit. The pot must be blazing hot to generate an initial burst of steam.

Remove the chilled dough from the refrigerator, turn it out onto a piece of parchment paper, and score the top with a sharp razor blade or bread lame. Make one decisive cut along the length of the loaf, about half an inch deep. Lower the dough into the hot Dutch oven, cover, and bake for twenty minutes.

After twenty minutes, carefully remove the lid and drop the oven temperature to 450 degrees Fahrenheit. Bake for another fifteen to twenty minutes until the crust turns a deep, burnished brown and an instant-read thermometer inserted into the center registers 210 degrees Fahrenheit.

Lift the bread from the pot and place it on a wire cooling rack. Wait at least two hours before slicing so the interior can finish setting and the crust can crisp. If you cannot finish the entire loaf, store it cut-side down on a cutting board for two days; after that, slices freeze well in a sealed bag.

No machine is required. A large bowl, a spatula, a sharp blade, and a hot pot are enough. The wild yeast in your starter will leaven the bread if you give it time, warmth, and gentle handling.
