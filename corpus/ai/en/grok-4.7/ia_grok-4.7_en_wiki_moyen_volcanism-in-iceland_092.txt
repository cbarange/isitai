Iceland lies astride the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, where the Eurasian and North American plates diverge, and above a mantle plume that supplies excess heat and magma. This combination produces one of the most active volcanic regions on Earth. Roughly thirty volcanic systems are recognized, many marked by central volcanoes, fissure swarms, and extensive lava fields composed largely of basalt.

Eruptions occur on average every few years. Historical events include the Laki fissure eruption of 1783–1784, which released large volumes of lava and sulfur gases and contributed to widespread climatic and agricultural disruption, and the 2010 eruption of Eyjafjallajökull, whose ash cloud disrupted transatlantic aviation. Other notable centers include Hekla, Katla, Grímsvötn, Bárðarbunga, Askja, and Krafla. Since 2021, repeated fissure eruptions on the Reykjanes Peninsula, including at Fagradalsfjall and near Sundhnúkur, have renewed activity in an area that had been quiet for centuries, according to monitoring by the Icelandic Meteorological Office and geological surveys.

Volcanism takes several forms. Effusive basaltic eruptions build shields and lava plains, while more explosive events occur where magma interacts with ice or groundwater. Subglacial eruptions can melt ice and generate jökulhlaups, sudden glacial outburst floods that threaten infrastructure. Icelanders have long adapted to these hazards through monitoring networks, hazard zoning, and evacuation planning, while also harnessing geothermal heat from volcanic systems for electricity and district heating. Research by institutions such as the University of Iceland continues to refine understanding of magma supply, eruption forecasting, and the interaction of the ridge and plume.
