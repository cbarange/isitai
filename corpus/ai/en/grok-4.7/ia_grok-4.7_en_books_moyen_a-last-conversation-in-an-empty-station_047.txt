They sat on the only bench that still faced the tracks, coats drawn close though the air had gone mild. The board above them had stopped naming places; a single bulb hummed and went dark, then hummed again, as if deciding whether the night was worth illuminating.

“I kept the ticket,” she said. “The one from the first time. It’s soft at the folds.”

He looked at the rails, bright where the last rain had settled. “You could still use it.”

“Not for where it says.”

A wind came down the platform and turned over a paper cup, sending it in a slow circle. Somewhere a door closed and did not open again. Their voices stayed low, not from secrecy but from the habit of rooms that had once been full.

“I thought there would be more to settle,” he said. “Bags. A time. Something that looked like leaving.”

“There isn’t.” She set her hand on the wood between them, not quite touching his. “Only this. And then the walk back, separately, so neither of us has to watch the other get small.”

He nodded. The cup stopped against his shoe. He did not move it.

“Say it plainly,” she said.

“I loved you in the stations more than in the houses. All that arriving. I never learned the staying.”

She closed her eyes once, the way people do when a train is late and they have already forgiven it. “Then let this be the last arrival.”

They stood. No whistle, no announcement, only the long empty shine of the rails and the sound of two sets of footsteps finding different exits, unhurried, exact, and done.
