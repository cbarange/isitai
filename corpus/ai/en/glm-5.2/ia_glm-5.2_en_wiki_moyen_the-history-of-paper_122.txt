Paper is generally understood to have originated in China, where the making of writing sheets from macerated plant fibres is traditionally attributed to Cai Lun, a court official of the Eastern Han dynasty, around 105 CE. Archaeological finds, including paper fragments from the second century BCE recovered at Fangmatan and Baqiao, indicate that rudimentary forms of paper predate his contribution, which scholars such as Tsien Tsuen-hsuin characterize as a standardization of existing practices rather than an invention. The technique spread gradually along trade routes, reaching Korea and Japan by the seventh century and, following the Abbasid victory at the Battle of Talas in 751, the Islamic world, where mills were established at Samarkand and, by the end of the eighth century, Baghdad. Production reached Islamic Spain by the eleventh century and Italy by the thirteenth, with centres such as Xàtiva and Fabriano introducing refinements including watermarks and gelatin sizing.

The spread of printing, particularly after Gutenberg's movable-type press in the mid-fifteenth century, greatly increased European demand. Until the nineteenth century, paper was made primarily from rags, but recurring shortages prompted experiments with other fibres. Mechanical wood-pulp grinding, attributed to Friedrich Gottlob Keller around 1845, and chemical pulping processes developed in subsequent decades made inexpensive paper possible.
