Do Social Networks Make Us Lonelier?

Never before have human beings been so connected. With a few taps on a screen, we can reach hundreds of acquaintances, watch the daily lives of strangers on the other side of the planet, and broadcast our own existence to an invisible audience. Yet at the same time, loneliness has become such a pressing public health issue that in 2018 the United Kingdom appointed its first Minister for Loneliness, and doctors routinely speak of an "epidemic" of isolation, particularly among the young — who happen to be social networks' heaviest users. This coincidence raises a troubling question: do the very tools that promise connection actually deepen our solitude? I will argue that they do — not inevitably, and not for everyone, but in the way most of us currently use them, social networks tend to manufacture loneliness rather than cure it.

The first reason is that social networks confuse the quantity of contact with the quality of relationships. A teenager may have eight hundred online "friends" and no one to call at two in the morning when things fall apart. Sociologists distinguish between "weak ties" — acquaintances, followers, contacts — and "strong ties," the few people who truly know us. Loneliness is determined by the second category, not the first. By flooding us with weak ties, networks create the illusion of a bustling social life while leaving its core empty. A study by researchers at the University of Pittsburgh found that young adults who spent the most time on social media reported significantly higher levels of perceived social isolation than light users — a result that inverts the platforms' advertising promises.

The second mechanism is social comparison. Social networks are not neutral mirrors of reality; they are showcases. Users publish the holiday, the party, the promotion — rarely the insomnia, the argument, or the ordinary Tuesday evening spent alone. Scrolling through this endless parade of curated happiness, we conclude, wrongly, that everyone else belongs somewhere and that we are the only ones eating dinner by ourselves. Psychologists call the resulting anxiety "the fear of missing out," and it is corrosive: feeling excluded, even from events we would not actually have enjoyed, makes us withdraw further — precisely the behaviour that deepens loneliness.

Third, social networks tend to replace richer forms of contact rather than complement them. An hour of scrolling often consumes the hour that might have been spent sharing a coffee with a friend. Moreover, digital communication is thin: stripped of tone, touch, eye contact and shared silence, it conveys information but not presence. As the researcher Sherry Turkle puts it, we risk being "alone together" — permanently in contact, yet rarely accompanied. The human body, which evolution equipped to find comfort in physical closeness, is not fooled by a heart emoji.

It would be dishonest, however, to present only one side of the case. For some people, social networks are a genuine lifeline: isolated elderly people, patients suffering from rare diseases, teenagers who are the only gay person in their small town, or emigrants living thousands of kilometres from their families. During the Covid-19 lockdowns, digital platforms kept friendships, schools and even family celebrations alive when physical contact was impossible. This suggests that the technology itself is not the culprit. Indeed, researchers who distinguish between "active" use — messaging, commenting, planning real meetings — and "passive" use, silently watching other people's lives, find that only the passive kind is consistently associated with lower well-being. The decisive factor, in other words, is not the network but the manner of using it.

Yet this nuance does not fully acquit the platforms, because they are engineered to encourage exactly the harmful pattern. Infinite feeds, autoplay and notifications are designed to maximise the time we spend on them, and time is best maximised by endless passive scrolling, not by writing a thoughtful message to one person or organising an actual meeting. When a tool's business model rewards our loneliest behaviour, blaming individual users alone seems remarkably generous to its designers.

In conclusion, social networks do not make us lonelier by nature, but they make us lonelier in practice. By substituting weak ties for friendship, comparison for community, and screens for presence, they often leave us more isolated than before we opened the app — while cleverly disguising the loss behind a wall of notifications. The remedy, then, is neither denial nor total digital abstinence, but conscious use: treating networks as what they can be — a means of arranging encounters, sustaining long-distance bonds and finding one's tribe — rather than as a substitute for human presence. Loneliness, in the end, is not cured by contacts; it is cured by company. And company still requires us, at some point, to log off.
