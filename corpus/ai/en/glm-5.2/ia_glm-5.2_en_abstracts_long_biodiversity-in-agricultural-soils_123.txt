Background: Soils host a large share of terrestrial biodiversity and underpin essential agroecosystem services, including organic matter decomposition, nutrient cycling, aggregate stabilization, carbon storage, and biological pest regulation. Agricultural intensification—characterized by deep tillage, simplified rotations, and high synthetic input use—is widely assumed to erode this diversity, yet empirical evidence remains fragmented: most studies address single taxonomic groups, single management factors, or short experimental durations, and rarely link community structure to measured functions under working farm conditions. Consequently, the relative importance of local management, soil properties, and landscape context in shaping multitaxon soil biodiversity remains poorly quantified, as does the trade-off—or lack thereof—between promoting belowground diversity and maintaining crop productivity. This study addressed three questions: (i) how does soil biodiversity, from microbes to macrofauna, vary across realistic gradients of management intensity in temperate croplands; (ii) which environmental drivers best explain multitaxon community structure; and (iii) does higher belowground diversity translate into enhanced ecosystem functioning and yield stability.

Methods: From 2021 to 2023 we surveyed 96 commercial farms in four temperate agricultural regions, stratified across four management systems (n = 24 each): conventional tillage, reduced tillage, organic farming, and conservation agriculture (no-till with permanent soil cover and diversified rotations). Fields were matched for soil type and cereal-dominated rotation to isolate management effects. Soil was sampled at 0–10 and 10–30 cm depths in spring and autumn. Biodiversity was assessed by 16S rRNA and ITS metabarcoding for bacteria and fungi, morphological and molecular identification of nematodes, hand-sorting and mustard extraction of earthworms, and Berlese extraction of collembolans and mites; surface-active ground beetles were recorded with pitfall traps to evaluate cross-realm coupling. Ecosystem functions included litter decomposition (tea bag index), extracellular enzyme activities (β-glucosidase, acid phosphatase, urease), basal respiration, potential nitrogen mineralization, and aggregate stability, alongside crop yield records. Soil organic carbon, pH, texture, nutrient status, bulk density, and the intensity of tillage, pesticide, and fertilizer use were quantified, and landscape composition (% semi-natural habitat within 250 m and 500 m) was derived from remote sensing. Data were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models (region and farm as random effects), PERMANOVA and variation partitioning for community composition, random forest variable-importance ranking of drivers, and piecewise structural equation modeling to test direct and indirect pathways from management through diversity to multifunctionality. Composite multitaxon diversity and standardized multifunctionality indices were computed to integrate across taxa and functions.

Results: We resolved approximately 18,400 bacterial and 5,100 fungal amplicon sequence variants, 61 nematode genera, 15 earthworm species, and 83 mesofaunal morphospecies. Management system explained 11–16% of compositional variation in all groups (PERMANOVA, p < 0.001), with soil organic carbon and tillage intensity emerging as the strongest predictors in random forest analyses; landscape effects were significant but weaker (4–7%), and strongest for surface-active and highly dispersive taxa. Conservation agriculture increased the multitaxon diversity index by 28% relative to conventional tillage, more than doubled total earthworm density (2.3×), raised anecic earthworm abundance 4.1-fold, and increased the fungal:bacterial ratio by 34%; organic systems showed intermediate gains, driven mainly by reduced biocide inputs. Diversity responses were strongest in the 0–10 cm layer, though fungal community differences persisted to 30 cm. Litter decomposition correlated with earthworm biomass and fungal richness (r = 0.52 and 0.47, p < 0.001), and enzyme activities increased with bacterial richness; multitaxon diversity and soil organic carbon explained 22% and 31% of variance, respectively, in the multifunctionality index, with a threshold-like increase in multifunctionality above approximately 2% organic carbon. Structural equation modeling indicated that management affected multifunctionality predominantly indirectly, through soil organic carbon and microbial diversity. Averaged over three years, yields in conservation systems were 4% lower than conventional (p = 0.09), whereas organic systems yielded 14% lower; interannual variability in nitrogen mineralization was 31% lower in high-diversity fields, supporting a diversity–stability relationship.

Conclusion: Under realistic field conditions, belowground biodiversity in temperate croplands is shaped primarily by the interacting effects of soil organic carbon and mechanical disturbance, and can be substantially restored within a few years by combining reduced tillage with permanent soil cover, with modest or negligible short-term yield penalties. Because diversity–function relationships were predominantly indirect—mediated by soil organic carbon—practices that build carbon stocks should be prioritized alongside biodiversity-targeted measures. Earthworm density, anecic abundance, and the fungal:bacterial ratio emerged as practical, cost-effective indicators for monitoring programs. Limitations include the temperate scope, the three-year duration, and the correlational design; long-term manipulative experiments across climates are needed to confirm causal pathways and assess yield trajectories beyond a decade. Overall, these findings support the integration of soil biodiversity indicators into agri-environment schemes and sustainable soil management frameworks, positioning belowground diversity as both a measurable outcome and a functional lever in agricultural landscapes.
