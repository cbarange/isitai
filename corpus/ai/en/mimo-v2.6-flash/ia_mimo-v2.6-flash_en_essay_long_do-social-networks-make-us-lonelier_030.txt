Do Social Networks Make Us Lonelier?

In the digital age, social media platforms have become an integral part of our daily lives. With billions of users worldwide, platforms such as Instagram, Facebook, and TikTok promise instant connection and a sense of belonging. Yet paradoxically, a growing body of research suggests that the more we engage with social networks, the lonelier we may feel. While social media is often portrayed as a tool that brings people closer together, I argue that it actually contributes to loneliness by fostering superficial relationships, encouraging unhealthy social comparison, and replacing meaningful face-to-face interactions with shallow digital exchanges.

First and foremost, social networks tend to create a quantity-over-quality problem in our relationships. Having hundreds or even thousands of online "friends" may seem like proof of a rich social life, but these connections are often thin and surface-level. A "like" on a photo or a brief comment under a post is a far cry from a genuine conversation that shows real interest and empathy. Over time, this illusion of closeness can leave users feeling disconnected, because the relationships they maintain online rarely provide the emotional depth that human beings need to feel truly understood. As the psychologist Robin Dunbar has pointed out, humans can only maintain a limited number of meaningful relationships, and social networks do not change this biological constraint. Instead, they merely stretch our attention across a wider but shallower network, leaving us with fewer truly intimate bonds.

Furthermore, social networks are powerful engines of social comparison, which is one of the most well-documented causes of loneliness. On platforms like Instagram and TikTok, people are constantly exposed to curated versions of other people's lives — their holidays, achievements, and perfect relationships. This creates a relentless pressure to measure up, and those who feel they fall short often withdraw socially rather than reach out for genuine support. A study published in the *Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology* in 2018 found that limiting social media use to thirty minutes per day significantly reduced feelings of loneliness and depression among young adults. This evidence strongly suggests that the constant exposure to idealised lifestyles on social networks does not inspire connection; on the contrary, it deepens a sense of isolation and inadequacy.

In addition, the time we spend on social networks inevitably comes at the expense of real-world interactions. Scrolling through feeds, responding to messages, and creating content for an online audience can consume hours of our day — hours that could otherwise be spent with friends and family. Although digital communication can certainly complement personal relationships, it should never replace them. When people substitute a face-to-face meeting with a video call or a text message, they lose access to non-verbal cues such as body language, tone of voice, and physical presence. These cues are essential for building trust and empathy, and their absence in digital exchanges often results in misunderstandings and a sense that conversations are incomplete. The result is a vicious cycle: people feel lonely, turn to social networks for comfort, spend less time with real people, and end up feeling even lonelier than before.

Admittedly, social networks are not entirely harmful. They can help people maintain long-distance friendships, provide a platform for marginalised voices, and offer communities of support for those with shared experiences or challenges. For someone living in a remote area or dealing with a rare illness, online groups can be a lifeline. However, these benefits only materialise when social media is used mindfully and in moderation. The problem arises when passive consumption and compulsive scrolling replace active, intentional engagement with both online and offline communities.

In conclusion, while social networks have the potential to connect people across distances and cultures, they more often contribute to loneliness than to genuine companionship. By creating an illusion of closeness, encouraging unhealthy comparison, and displacing meaningful face-to-face interactions, these platforms leave users feeling isolated despite being more "connected" than ever before. To avoid falling into this trap, we must learn to use social media as a supplement to — rather than a substitute for — real human connection. Only by being conscious of how and why we use these tools can we ensure that technology serves our well-being instead of undermining it.
