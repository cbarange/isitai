The parcel arrived on a Thursday morning when the rain had not yet decided whether to fall, and Émile Rostain set it down between a stack of Flauberts and a crate of unsorted secondhand purchases, meaning to deal with it later. It had no return address — only his shop's name, written in a hand that was precise and slightly tremulous, as though the writer's arm had been very old or very tired.

He did not open it until the afternoon lull, when the shop fell quiet and the only sound was the ticking of the grandfather clock that had never once been wound but that everyone, including Émile, silently relied upon. The manuscript was bound in plain grey paper, tied with a single thread. Inside, the pages were typewritten — not printed, typed, each character bearing the small violence of a metal key striking ribbon.

The first sentence held him still. It described, in language of almost unbearable intimacy, a room he had stood in once, thirty years earlier, in a city he had not named to anyone alive. He read two pages, then set the manuscript down, aware that his hands were trembling not with cold but with something closer to recognition, as if the words had come from the inside of his own memory, arriving like a guest he had once invited and then forgotten to expect.
