The immune system is the network of organs, tissues, cells, and molecules that protects an organism against infection and other forms of biological harm. In vertebrates, it is commonly described as comprising two interacting layers: innate immunity, which provides rapid, broadly targeted responses, and adaptive immunity, which develops more slowly and recognizes specific molecular features of pathogens. These systems work together to detect microbes such as bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites, eliminate infected or damaged cells, and regulate repair processes. The immune system also distinguishes self from non-self, a capacity that is essential to preventing attacks on the body’s own tissues while maintaining responsiveness to external threats.

Innate immunity includes physical and chemical barriers, including the skin, mucous membranes, stomach acid, and antimicrobial peptides. When these barriers are breached, resident immune cells detect conserved microbial patterns through receptors that have evolved to recognize features common to many pathogens. Key innate cells include macrophages, neutrophils, dendritic cells, natural killer cells, and mast cells. These cells can engulf microbes, release inflammatory signals, recruit additional immune cells, and destroy infected cells. The complement system, a group of plasma proteins, supports these responses by marking pathogens for destruction, promoting inflammation, and directly disrupting microbial membranes. Inflammation is a hallmark of innate responses and serves to contain infection, although excessive or prolonged inflammation can damage host tissues.

Adaptive immunity is mediated primarily by lymphocytes, especially B cells and T cells, which express highly diverse receptors generated through genetic recombination. B cells produce antibodies, soluble proteins that bind specific antigens and can neutralize toxins, block viral entry, or mark pathogens for destruction by other immune cells. T cells recognize antigens displayed on cell surfaces by major histocompatibility complex molecules. Helper T cells coordinate immune responses by secreting cytokines that activate macrophages, B cells, and other lymphocytes, while cytotoxic T cells kill infected or abnormal cells. Regulatory T cells help limit immune activation and maintain tolerance to self-antigens. Because adaptive responses depend on clonal selection and expansion, they become stronger and more specific after repeated exposure to the same antigen.

Immune memory is one of the most important features of adaptive immunity. After an infection is cleared, a subset of antigen-specific lymphocytes persists as memory cells, allowing a faster and more effective response upon re-exposure. This principle underlies vaccination, in which antigens or attenuated pathogens are introduced to stimulate protective immunity without causing disease. Immunological memory also explains why some infections confer long-lasting protection and why booster doses may be needed for certain vaccines. At the same time, the immune system must avoid excessive reactivity. Tolerance mechanisms eliminate or suppress lymphocytes that strongly recognize self-antigens, primarily in the thymus for T cells and bone marrow for B cells, with additional controls operating in peripheral tissues.

The immune system is distributed throughout the body and includes primary lymphoid organs, where immune cells develop, and secondary lymphoid organs, where responses are initiated. Bone marrow produces blood cells and is the site of B-cell maturation, while the thymus supports T-cell development. Secondary sites include lymph nodes, the spleen, tonsils, and mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue, which sample antigens from tissues, blood, or mucosal surfaces. Lymphatic vessels transport immune cells and antigen-bearing dendritic cells to lymph nodes, where interactions with lymphocytes determine whether an immune response is activated. The precise regulation of cell migration, signaling, and antigen presentation is therefore central to effective immunity.

Clinical disorders arise when immune function is insufficient, excessive, or misdirected. Immunodeficiency can result from genetic defects, infections such as HIV, malnutrition, aging, or medical treatments that suppress immunity, increasing susceptibility to infections and certain cancers. Autoimmune diseases occur when tolerance fails and immune responses attack self-tissues, as in type 1 diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, and multiple sclerosis. Allergic diseases reflect inappropriate responses to harmless environmental antigens, involving antibodies of the immunoglobulin E class and mast-cell activation. Transplant rejection is driven by recognition of foreign histocompatibility molecules, requiring immunosuppressive therapy in many cases. Conversely, immune mechanisms are increasingly used in medicine, including monoclonal antibodies, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and cell-based therapies designed to enhance antitumor immunity.

Research in immunology continues to clarify how immune cells communicate, how microbiota influence immune development, and how immune responses vary across age, sex, and genetic background. These insights inform vaccine design, treatments for inflammatory and autoimmune disease, and strategies to improve cancer therapy, while also highlighting the immune system’s role as a dynamic interface between host biology and the environment.
