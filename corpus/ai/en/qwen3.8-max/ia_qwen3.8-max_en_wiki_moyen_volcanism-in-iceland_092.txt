Volcanism in Iceland results from the interaction between the Mid-Atlantic Ridge and the Iceland hotspot, which together produce frequent eruptions across the island and its surrounding shelf. Most activity is basaltic and occurs along fissure systems linked to central volcanoes, though silica-rich eruptions and subglacial events also occur. The country’s volcanic zones include the Eastern, Western, Northern, and Reykjanes Volcanic Zones, as well as the offshore Tjörnes Fracture Zone. Major systems include Katla, Hekla, Grímsvötn, Bárðarbunga, Askja, Krafla, and the Reykjanes Peninsula. Eruptions can be effusive, producing extensive lava fields, or explosive, especially when magma interacts with ice or water; the 1783–1784 Laki eruption and the 2010 Eyjafjallajökull event illustrate these contrasting hazards. Icelandic volcanism generates jökulhlaups, ash plumes, sulfur dioxide emissions, and seismic swarms, which are monitored by the Icelandic Meteorological Office, the University of Iceland’s Institute of Earth Sciences, and the Department of Civil Protection and Emergency Management. Historical records, tephrochronology, and geodetic measurements are used to assess eruption recurrence and magma movement. Volcanic activity also supports geothermal energy production, district heating, and research into magma–ice interaction and volcanic risk management.
