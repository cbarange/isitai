Background: Carbon and low-alloy steels are the dominant materials for ships, ports, offshore platforms, and coastal pipelines because of their strength, weldability, and relatively low cost, but their durability in marine environments is limited by electrochemical corrosion accelerated by chloride, dissolved oxygen, temperature, hydrodynamics, and microbial activity. Corrosion losses are spatially heterogeneous: the atmospheric zone is controlled by wetting time and airborne salts, the splash and tidal zones experience frequent oxygen replenishment and cyclic immersion, and the submerged zone is influenced by biofilm development, sulfate-reducing bacteria, sediment chemistry, and cathodic protection efficiency. Despite extensive work on individual factors, practical design still requires quantitative relationships among environmental variables, steel surface films, localized attack, and the performance of combined coating/cathodic-protection systems. This study therefore aimed to determine the simultaneous effects of temperature, chloride concentration, dissolved oxygen, and flow velocity on carbon-steel corrosion, identify the solid corrosion products and microbiological contributors responsible for localized damage, and evaluate a zinc-rich epoxy coating coupled with Al-Zn-In sacrificial anodes under long-term marine exposure.

Methods: Flat coupons of structural carbon steel were cleaned, weighed, and exposed at two coastal sites for 6, 12, and 24 months in the atmospheric, splash, tidal, and submerged zones. Mass-loss rates were calculated according to ASTM G1 after mechanical removal and acid pickling, while pit depth was measured by optical profilometry and expressed as a pitting factor. Parallel electrochemical tests were performed in artificial and filtered natural seawater. Open-circuit potential, potentiodynamic polarization, and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy were carried out at 5, 15, 25, and 35 °C; solution resistance, charge-transfer resistance, constant-phase-element parameters, and corrosion current density were extracted using equivalent-circuit fitting. Dissolved oxygen, pH, salinity, sulfide, and biofilm abundance were monitored, and plate counts plus ATP assays were used to estimate microbial activity. Corrosion layers were characterized by scanning electron microscopy, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction, and Raman spectroscopy. A response-surface design quantified interactions among temperature, chloride, oxygen, and flow velocity. Finally, coated panels with and without sacrificial anodes were subjected to cyclic immersion, cathodic disbondment testing, and anode mass-consumption measurements.

Results: Degradation was strongly zone dependent. After 24 months, average corrosion rates were approximately 0.05 mm/year in the atmospheric zone, 0.13 mm/year in the submerged zone, 0.27 mm/year in the tidal zone, and 0.49 mm/year in the splash zone. The highest pitting factor, about 4.7, occurred in the tidal zone rather than in the continuously submerged zone, where calcareous deposits partially suppressed penetration. Raising the bulk water temperature from 5 to 25 °C increased the corrosion current density from about 9 to 55 µA/cm², corresponding to an apparent temperature coefficient near 1.9; the effect weakened at 35 °C because oxygen solubility declined even though reaction kinetics remained high. Polarization measurements showed active corrosion potentials, generally 60 to 140 mV more negative after long exposure, and anodic/cathodic Tafel slopes consistent with mixed charge-transfer and mass-transfer control. Impedance spectra evolved from one dominant time constant for bare steel to two time constants after biofilm and scale formation. Charge-transfer resistance fell from roughly 1850 to 310 Ω·cm² over 24 months in the splash zone and to about 520 Ω·cm² in quiescent submerged conditions, while the non-ideal capacitance exponent remained below 0.9, indicating heterogeneous, porous films. Surface analyses identified lepidocrocite, goethite, akaganéite, and magnetite/maghemite, with calcite and aragonite in the more protected submerged deposits. Chloride was enriched near the metal/scale interface, whereas calcium, magnesium, iron, and sulfur were distributed through outer layers. Biofilms were most developed in the tidal and submerged zones; sulfide concentrations up to 18 mg/L and elevated ATP signals coincided with deeper, under-deposit-type attack. Flow velocities above 0.8 m/s removed weakly adherent scale, sustained high oxygen flux, and shifted corrosion from pitting-dominated to erosion-assisted mass loss. The response-surface model reproduced measured rates with R² = 0.94 and indicated that oxygen and temperature were the strongest individual drivers, while chloride×flow and oxygen×temperature interactions were also significant. The zinc-rich epoxy coating reduced cathodic protection current demand by approximately 78%. Panels protected at −0.90 to −1.05 V versus Ag/AgCl maintained steel potentials within the usual protection window, showed only 4.2 mm average disbondment after 24 months, and consumed Al-Zn-In anodes at about 4.1 kg/A·year. Unprotected coated controls failed by blistering and filiform-like attack at scratches within 12 months.

Conclusion: In marine service, carbon-steel corrosion is not governed by average immersion conditions alone; the splash and tidal zones can be more damaging than permanent submergence because cyclic wetting concentrates chloride and maximizes oxygen access. Temperature accelerates corrosion mainly through oxygen transport and reaction kinetics until oxygen solubility becomes limiting, whereas high flow removes protective scale and promotes erosion-corrosion. Microbial films and sulfide accumulation localize attack beneath deposits, explaining why pitting severity can exceed uniform mass-loss predictions. Protective calcareous layers form most effectively in oxygenated, low-flow submerged seawater, but their benefit is unreliable where mechanical damage, erosion, or MIC occurs. A zinc-rich epoxy coating combined with properly potential-controlled Al-Zn-In anodes substantially reduced current demand and disbondment, indicating that durable marine protection should integrate zone-specific design, inspection of occluded areas, hydrodynamic control, and cathodic protection criteria verified against local temperature and oxygen conditions.
