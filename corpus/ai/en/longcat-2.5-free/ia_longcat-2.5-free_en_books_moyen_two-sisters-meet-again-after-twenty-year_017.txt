The rain had stopped an hour before Lena arrived, but the lilac bushes along the fence still dripped, as though the evening itself were settling into the grass. Mara watched from the kitchen window, drying her hands on a towel she had no further use for.

Twenty years had made a stranger of her sister's walk. Lena came up the path the way she had always come—too quickly, as if the door might close—but her coat was a city coat now, dark and narrow, and she carried a small suitcase with the careful grip of someone who had learned to travel light.

At the door they did not embrace. Mara stepped aside. Lena stepped in. The hallway smelled of thyme and old wood, and of the rain tracked in on unfamiliar shoes.

"You cut your hair," Mara said.

"You didn't."

They stood among the things that had outlasted them: the bent umbrella stand, the mirror with its crack running like a river on a map, the clock their mother had wound without ever once asking the time. Lena set down her suitcase. It made no sound at all, and somehow that was the thing that undid them—not the hair, not the coat, not the years folded small as letters in a drawer.

"I would have come," Lena said, "sooner."

"I know," said Mara. And then, because it was true, and because it was the first true thing either had said in twenty years: "Stay for the lilacs. They're only here a week."
