Tides are the long-period cyclic rise and fall of oceans and other large water bodies, driven principally by the gravitational attraction of the Moon and, to a lesser degree, the Sun, modified by Earth’s rotation, orbital geometry, and coastal and seabed topography. Because the Moon’s pull is stronger on the side of Earth nearest it and weaker on the far side, water is differentially displaced into two bulges; as the planet rotates, most coastal locations pass through both bulges and the intervening lows, producing approximately two high and two low tides each lunar day of 24 hours 50 minutes. Where local basin shape suppresses one component, the pattern may instead be mainly diurnal or mixed.

Solar gravity contributes roughly 46 percent of the lunar effect. When Sun, Earth, and Moon align near new and full moon, a configuration called syzygy, their forces reinforce to produce higher spring tides; at first and last quarters, known as quadrature, the weaker neap tides occur. The highest ranges, exceeding 16 metres in the Bay of Fundy, result from funnel-shaped coasts and resonant amplification; many enclosed seas show only small oscillations. Besides water movements, solid-Earth, atmospheric, and lake tides occur. Tidal friction gradually transfers angular momentum from Earth to the Moon, lengthening the day and increasing the Moon’s orbital distance by about 3.8 centimetres per year.

Systematic description began with Newton’s equilibrium theory in 1687 and was advanced by Laplace’s dynamic theory in 1775; modern predictions use harmonic analysis of astronomically determined constituents. Tidal data inform navigation
