Background: Indoor air quality (IAQ) in schools is a growing public health concern, as children and adolescents spend a significant proportion of their daily lives in educational buildings. Emerging evidence suggests that exposure to indoor pollutants—including volatile organic compounds (VOCs), particulate matter (PM2.5 and PM10), carbon dioxide (CO2), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), formaldehyde, biological contaminants such as mould spores and bacteria, and radon—may adversely affect respiratory health, cognitive performance, academic achievement, and overall well-being. Schools in many regions are often housed in older buildings with inadequate or poorly maintained ventilation systems, leading to elevated pollutant concentrations and insufficient fresh air supply. Although international guidelines from the World Health Organization (WHO) and national agencies recommend CO2 levels below 1000 ppm and PM2.5 concentrations below 15 µg/m³ (annual mean), compliance in school settings remains inconsistent. Previous studies have investigated IAQ parameters in individual schools or small clusters, but comprehensive multi-site assessments that simultaneously measure physical, chemical, and biological indicators while considering building characteristics, occupancy patterns, and outdoor air quality remain limited. Understanding the relative contributions of these factors is essential for designing evidence-based interventions that protect the health and learning environment of schoolchildren.

Methods: This study employed a prospective, cross-sectional design across 24 primary and secondary schools in a temperate urban setting. Schools were selected using stratified random sampling to represent a range of building ages (pre-1970, 1970–2000, post-2000), ventilation types (natural, mechanical, hybrid), and socioeconomic contexts. Over a six-month monitoring period encompassing both heating and non-heating seasons, continuous real-time measurements of CO2, temperature, relative humidity, PM2.5, and PM10 were recorded in 72 classrooms at 15-minute intervals using calibrated reference monitors. Integrated 48-hour air samples were collected for formaldehyde and selected VOCs using sorbent tubes and analysed by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS). Radon concentrations were measured over a full academic week using alpha-track detectors. Bioaerosol sampling was performed using impaction methods on culture media for total bacteria and mould enumeration. Outdoor reference stations simultaneously recorded ambient PM2.5, NO2, and meteorological data. Ventilation rates were estimated via CO2 decay analysis and tracer gas methods where feasible. Building characteristics, cleaning practices, classroom occupancy, window-opening behaviour, and use of consumer products (e.g., whiteboard markers, disinfectants) were documented through standardised questionnaires and site audits. Children's respiratory symptoms were assessed using an adapted version of the International Study of Asthma and Allergies in Childhood (ISAAC) questionnaire, distributed to 3,842 pupils with a response rate of 78.4%. Statistical analyses included descriptive summaries, multivariate linear and logistic regression models to identify predictors of pollutant concentrations and associations with health outcomes, and mixed-effects models to account for clustering of classrooms within schools. Adjustments were made for confounders including age, sex, parental smoking, allergen sensitisation history, and season.

Results: CO2 concentrations exceeded the 1000 ppm threshold in 68% of monitored classrooms during occupied periods, with peak values reaching 2,650 ppm in naturally ventilated, pre-1970 buildings. Mean indoor PM2.5 levels were 14.2 ± 8.7 µg/m³, and 41% of classrooms recorded daily averages above 15 µg/m³. Indoor PM2.5 was significantly correlated with outdoor levels (r = 0.62, p < 0.001) but was also elevated during cleaning activities and arts/crafts lessons (1.8-fold increase, p < 0.01). Formaldehyde was detected in all classrooms, with geometric means of 21.3 µg/m³; levels exceeded the WHO guideline of 100 µg/m³ in only 4% of rooms, but were highest in recently refurbished classrooms with composite wood furnishings. Radon concentrations were below 300 Bq/m³ in all schools, though 11% of ground-floor classrooms exceeded 100 Bq/m³. Mould was identified in 29% of classrooms, predominantly *Cladosporium* and *Penicillium* species, and was associated with visible dampness (OR = 4.7, 95% CI: 2.9–7.5). Multivariate analysis revealed that inadequate ventilation rate (< 6 L/s per person) was the strongest independent predictor of elevated CO2 (β = 0.71, p < 0.001) and was associated with increased odds of reported wheeze among pupils (adjusted OR = 1.54, 95% CI: 1.18–2.01). Every 500 ppm increase in time-weighted CO2 was associated with a 0.31-point decrease in standardised reading scores on a sub-sample assessment (p = 0.03). Classrooms in mechanically ventilated, post-2000 buildings maintained the lowest pollutant levels but occasionally showed higher VOC emissions due to recent material installations.

Conclusion: Indoor air quality in the surveyed schools was suboptimal, with chronic elevation of CO2 and recurrent PM2.5 exceedances driven primarily by insufficient ventilation. Measurable associations between poor IAQ and respiratory symptoms as well as reduced cognitive performance underscore the public health and educational significance of these findings. The results highlight that building age, ventilation design, occupancy density, and in-classroom activities are key determinants of exposure. Targeted interventions—such as upgrading ventilation systems, implementing occupancy-aware ventilation schedules, selecting low-emission materials and cleaning products, and raising occupant awareness about window-opening practices—are recommended to improve IAQ and safeguard children's health and learning outcomes. Future longitudinal research should evaluate the efficacy and cost-effectiveness of these interventions in real-world school environments.
