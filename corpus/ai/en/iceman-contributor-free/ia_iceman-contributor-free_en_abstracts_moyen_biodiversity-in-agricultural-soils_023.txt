Background: Agricultural intensification has led to significant declines in soil biodiversity, threatening essential ecosystem functions such as nutrient cycling, carbon sequestration, and pest regulation. Understanding how different management practices influence belowground communities is critical for developing sustainable farming systems that maintain soil health and productivity.

Methods: This study assessed soil biodiversity across 60 agricultural sites under conventional, organic, and conservation tillage systems over two growing seasons. Soil samples were collected from the top 20 cm of each site and analyzed for microbial diversity using 16S rRNA and ITS sequencing. Macro- and mesofauna were identified through morphological and molecular techniques. Soil physicochemical properties, including pH, organic matter content, and nutrient levels, were measured to evaluate their relationships with biodiversity metrics.

Results: Organic and conservation tillage systems exhibited significantly higher microbial richness and faunal abundance compared to conventional systems (p < 0.05). Soil organic matter content and reduced tillage intensity were the strongest predictors of biodiversity, accounting for 42% of the observed variation. Fungal diversity was 35% higher in organically managed soils, and earthworm abundance was threefold greater in conservation tillage plots than in conventionally managed fields.

Conclusion: These findings demonstrate that sustainable agricultural practices, particularly organic management and conservation tillage, enhance soil biodiversity and support key ecosystem functions. Adopting such practices can improve soil resilience, reduce reliance on external inputs, and promote long-term agricultural sustainability.
