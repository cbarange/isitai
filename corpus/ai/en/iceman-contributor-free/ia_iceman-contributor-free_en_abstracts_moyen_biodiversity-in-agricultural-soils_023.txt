**Background:** Agricultural intensification has led to significant declines in soil biodiversity, threatening essential ecosystem services such as nutrient cycling, pest regulation, and carbon sequestration. Understanding how different farming practices influence soil biota is critical for developing sustainable agricultural systems that maintain both productivity and ecological resilience.

**Methods:** A comparative assessment was conducted across 60 agricultural sites representing conventional, organic, and conservation tillage systems. Soil samples were collected from the top 20 cm layer and analyzed for microbial, nematode, and arthropod diversity using high-throughput DNA sequencing and morphological identification. Key physicochemical properties, including soil organic carbon, pH, and nutrient content, were measured to evaluate their relationships with biodiversity indices.

**Results:** Conservation tillage and organic farming systems exhibited significantly higher species richness and Shannon diversity indices compared to conventional systems (p < 0.05). Microbial biomass and nematode abundance were positively correlated with soil organic carbon content (r = 0.72 and r = 0.65, respectively). Arthropod diversity was notably higher in fields characterized by reduced pesticide application and increased crop rotation complexity. Overall, management practices that minimized soil disturbance and chemical inputs supported more diverse and functionally resilient soil communities.

**Conclusion:** These findings demonstrate that sustainable agricultural practices, particularly conservation tillage and organic management, enhance soil biodiversity and its associated ecosystem functions. Integrating biodiversity-friendly strategies into agricultural policies and management frameworks is essential for preserving soil health and ensuring long-term agricultural productivity.
