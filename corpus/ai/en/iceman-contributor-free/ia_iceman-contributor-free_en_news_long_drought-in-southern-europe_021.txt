Large swathes of southern Europe are confronting severe drought conditions as prolonged rainfall deficits and above-average temperatures strain water reserves, disrupt agricultural production and force authorities to impose restrictions on water use. From the Iberian Peninsula to southern Italy and Greece, governments are implementing emergency measures while scientists warn that such episodes are becoming more frequent and intense as the climate warms.

Data from the European Drought Observatory, part of the European Commission’s Copernicus programme, show that significant portions of Spain, Portugal, Italy, Greece and southern France have experienced warning or alert-level drought conditions in recent months. Soil moisture levels in many regions remain well below seasonal averages, while river flows and reservoir volumes have declined following consecutive seasons of insufficient rainfall combined with periods of extreme heat.

Spain has been among the most severely affected countries. Reservoir levels in Catalonia fell below 16 percent of capacity in early 2024, prompting regional authorities to declare a drought emergency and introduce strict limits on water use for agriculture, industry and households. Restrictions included reductions in irrigation for crops, limits on water use for livestock, and bans on filling private swimming pools and washing cars with drinking water. Although rainfall in the spring improved conditions in some areas, water reserves in parts of the northeast and south remain under pressure, and officials have cautioned that long-term structural shortages persist.

In Sicily, the Italian government declared a state of emergency in early 2024 after months of below-average rainfall depleted reservoirs and threatened drinking water supplies. Water rationing was introduced in several municipalities, with some towns receiving supplies on alternating days. Farmers across the island reported significant losses to wheat, citrus and olive crops, while livestock producers faced rising costs for feed and water transport. Other regions, including Sardinia and parts of southern mainland Italy, have also reported declining reservoir levels and stressed water systems.

Portugal and Greece have faced similar challenges. Portuguese authorities have monitored critically low water levels in the Algarve and Alentejo regions, where agriculture and tourism place heavy seasonal demands on limited supplies. In Greece, reduced rainfall and high summer temperatures have affected water availability on several islands, leading to increased reliance on desalination and water shipments. Local authorities have urged residents and businesses to curb consumption, particularly during peak tourist months.

The agricultural sector has borne a substantial share of the impact. Southern Europe is a major producer of olives, grapes, citrus fruits and cereals, crops that are highly sensitive to prolonged dry conditions. Farming associations across the region have reported lower yields and rising production costs, with some growers forced to abandon fields or reduce planting. According to industry groups, consecutive years of drought have contributed to volatility in the production of olive oil and wine, two of the region’s most important agricultural exports. The European Commission has allowed member states to provide advance payments and emergency support to affected farmers under the Common Agricultural Policy.

Environmental consequences are also mounting. Low river levels have disrupted freshwater ecosystems, reduced hydropower generation and increased concentrations of pollutants in shrinking water bodies. Prolonged dry conditions have heightened the risk of wildfires, which have become more frequent and severe across the Mediterranean basin. Civil protection agencies in several countries have extended wildfire alert periods and increased surveillance and preparedness measures.

Governments are responding with a combination of short-term restrictions and longer-term investments. Measures include expanding desalination capacity, improving wastewater recycling, repairing leaking distribution networks and promoting more efficient irrigation techniques. Spain’s national water plan, updated in recent years, places greater emphasis on reuse and demand management, while Italy has allocated funds to upgrade ageing water infrastructure and build new reservoirs.

Climate scientists say the trend is consistent with broader changes observed across the Mediterranean, a region identified by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change as a climate change hotspot. Rising average temperatures increase evaporation rates and intensify dry spells, even in years when total rainfall does not decline dramatically.

“The Mediterranean is warming faster than the global average, and that is amplifying the severity and duration of droughts,” said a senior researcher at the Copernicus Climate Change Service. “Adapting water management systems to this new reality is no longer optional; it is essential for safeguarding food production, ecosystems and public water supplies.”

As southern European countries prepare for the coming summer, authorities remain cautious. While periods of rainfall can provide temporary relief, hydrologists and policymakers alike stress that recurring deficits underscore the need for sustained investment and coordinated regional strategies to manage an increasingly scarce resource.
