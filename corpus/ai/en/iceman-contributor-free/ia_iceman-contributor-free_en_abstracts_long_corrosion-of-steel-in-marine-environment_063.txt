Background

Steel remains one of the most widely used structural materials in marine infrastructure, including offshore platforms, ship hulls, pipelines, port facilities, and coastal reinforcement systems, owing to its high strength, versatility, and cost-effectiveness. However, marine environments are among the most aggressive settings for metallic materials, exposing steel to a complex combination of physical, chemical, and biological stressors. High chloride concentrations, dissolved oxygen, fluctuating temperatures, variable salinity, humidity, tidal cycles, and biofouling collectively accelerate electrochemical degradation processes. In these conditions, steel is susceptible to multiple forms of corrosion, including uniform corrosion, pitting, crevice corrosion, galvanic corrosion, and microbiologically influenced corrosion (MIC).

The corrosion of steel in marine environments poses significant economic, environmental, and safety challenges. Structural deterioration can lead to reduced load-bearing capacity, compromised serviceability, unplanned maintenance, and, in severe cases, catastrophic failures. The global costs associated with corrosion-related inspection, repair, and replacement are substantial, underscoring the need for a comprehensive understanding of corrosion mechanisms and influencing factors. Although considerable research has been conducted, the interplay between environmental conditions, material properties, and corrosion kinetics across different marine exposure zones remains complex and context-dependent. This study aims to systematically investigate the corrosion behavior of carbon steel in marine environments, characterize the mechanisms governing degradation, and assess the influence of exposure conditions on corrosion rates and product formation.

Methods

A combined field and laboratory investigation was conducted over a 24-month period to evaluate the corrosion performance of low-carbon steel (ASTM A36) in marine conditions. Standardized steel coupons were prepared, degreased, weighed, and deployed at a coastal test site in three distinct exposure zones: atmospheric (splash), tidal, and fully submerged (immersion). Environmental parameters, including seawater temperature, salinity, pH, dissolved oxygen concentration, and tidal fluctuations, were continuously monitored throughout the exposure period.

Corrosion rates were determined at intervals of 3, 6, 12, and 24 months using gravimetric weight-loss measurements in accordance with ASTM G1 standards. Following exposure, corrosion products were removed, and mass loss was used to calculate average corrosion rates. Surface morphology and microstructural characteristics of the corroded specimens were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). The crystalline composition of corrosion products was identified through X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis.

To complement field observations, electrochemical tests were performed in simulated seawater solutions. Potentiodynamic polarization and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) were employed to evaluate corrosion current density, polarization resistance, and electrochemical behavior. Additionally, selected specimens from the submerged zone were analyzed for microbial activity through culturing techniques and biofilm characterization to assess the contribution of MIC to overall degradation.

Results

The results revealed significant variations in corrosion behavior across the three exposure zones. After 24 months, the highest average corrosion rate was observed in the splash zone (0.45 mm/year), followed by the tidal zone (0.38 mm/year) and the fully submerged zone (0.22 mm/year). The elevated corrosion rates in the splash and tidal zones were attributed to frequent wet-dry cycles, which promoted oxygen diffusion and increased chloride ion concentration at the steel surface, thereby accelerating electrochemical reactions.

Gravimetric data indicated a non-linear corrosion progression, with higher initial corrosion rates that gradually decreased over time. This trend was associated with the formation of rust layers that provided partial, albeit unstable, protection against further degradation. XRD analysis identified lepidocrocite (γ-FeOOH), goethite (α-FeOOH), magnetite (Fe₃O₄), and akaganeite (β-FeOOH) as the predominant corrosion products. Akaganeite was particularly prevalent in specimens exposed to chloride-rich conditions, reflecting its role in facilitating localized corrosion.

SEM-EDS analyses revealed porous and heterogeneous rust layers, with evidence of localized pitting and crevice attack, especially in the tidal zone. Electrochemical measurements supported the field findings, showing higher corrosion current densities and lower polarization resistance in specimens subjected to cyclic wetting and drying. Furthermore, microbial analysis of submerged specimens indicated the presence of sulfate-reducing bacteria and biofilm formation, suggesting that MIC contributed to localized sulfide generation and pit propagation. Seasonal increases in temperature and salinity were also correlated with elevated corrosion kinetics.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the corrosion of carbon steel in marine environments is strongly influenced by exposure zone, environmental conditions, and the composition of corrosion products. Splash and tidal zones exhibited the most severe degradation due to enhanced oxygen availability and chloride accumulation, while submerged conditions favored lower but persistent corrosion rates, partially influenced by microbial activity. The formation of complex, often non-protective rust layers and the occurrence of localized corrosion mechanisms highlight the vulnerability of steel in marine settings. These findings emphasize the need for zone-specific corrosion mitigation strategies, including appropriate material selection, protective coatings, cathodic protection, and regular monitoring. A comprehensive, environment-informed approach is essential to improve the durability, safety, and service life of steel structures in marine applications.
