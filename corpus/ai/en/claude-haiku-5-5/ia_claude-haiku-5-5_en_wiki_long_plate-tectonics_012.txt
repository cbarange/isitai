Plate tectonics is the scientific theory that Earth's outer shell is divided into a number of large, rigid slabs called tectonic plates, which move relative to one another over the underlying mantle. The theory accounts for the distribution of earthquakes, volcanoes, mountain ranges, and ocean basins, and it is the unifying framework of modern geology. Its development drew on observations from many fields over more than half a century.

The idea has roots in the early twentieth century. In 1912, the German meteorologist Alfred Wegener presented the hypothesis of continental drift, arguing that the continents had once been joined in a single landmass and had since separated. He supported this claim with the apparent fit of the coastlines of South America and Africa, with matching rock formations across oceans, and with the distribution of fossils such as *Glossopteris* and *Mesosaurus*. Wegener's proposal was widely rejected in his lifetime, mainly because he could not identify a force capable of moving continents through the oceanic crust. Many geophysicists of the period considered the continents to be fixed.

Renewed interest came after the Second World War, when systematic mapping of the ocean floor revealed extensive mid-ocean ridges, long submarine mountain chains with a central rift valley. Harry Hess (1962) proposed that new oceanic crust forms at these ridges and spreads outward, a process he called seafloor spreading. Independently, Robert Dietz (1961) reached a similar conclusion based on studies of the ocean floor. Further support came from the study of paleomagnetism. Fred Vine and Drummond Matthews (1963), building on earlier observations, showed that symmetrical stripes of alternating magnetic polarity on either side of the ridges matched the timing of reversals in Earth's magnetic field, which provided a quantitative test of seafloor spreading. Dan McKenzie and Robert Parker (1967) and W. Jason Morgan (1968) subsequently showed, using seismic and fault data, that the surface of the Earth could be described as a set of rigid plates moving on a sphere, which gave the theory its mathematical form.

In the modern formulation, the lithosphere, comprising the crust and the uppermost mantle, is roughly 100 kilometres thick in many places and overlies the more ductile asthenosphere. The lithosphere is broken into about seven major plates, including the Pacific, North American, Eurasian, African, Antarctic, Indo-Australian, and South American plates, together with a number of smaller ones. Plates move at rates typically between about 2 and 10 centimetres per year, roughly the speed at which fingernails grow. Their motion is measured directly by geodetic techniques, including the Global Positioning System and very long baseline interferometry, which confirm the rates inferred from magnetic and geological evidence.

Plate boundaries are classified by their relative motion. At divergent boundaries, such as the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, plates separate and new lithosphere is created. At convergent boundaries, plates collide; where oceanic lithosphere descends beneath another plate in a process called subduction, deep ocean trenches, volcanic arcs, and zones of intermediate and deep earthquakes form, as recorded in the Wadati–Benioff zones identified by seismologists in the 1950s. Where two continental plates converge, as in the Himalayas, the collision produces major mountain belts without significant subduction. At transform boundaries, plates slide horizontally past one another, as along the San Andreas Fault in California. J. Tuzo Wilson (1965) identified transform faults as a distinct class of boundary, and he later (1966) proposed that repeated cycles of ocean opening and closing could explain the assembly and breakup of supercontinents, a concept now known as the Wilson cycle.

The forces driving plate motion remain a subject of research, although the general principles are well established. Slab pull, the weight of cold, dense lithosphere sinking into the mantle at subduction zones, is widely regarded as the most important driver. Ridge push, the gravitational sliding of elevated lithosphere away from mid-ocean ridges, is also considered significant. Mantle convection provides the broader thermal context, but the relative contributions of these mechanisms and the detailed structure of mantle flow continue to be debated among geophysicists.

Plate tectonics is supported by a convergence of independent lines of evidence, including seafloor age mapping, paleomagnetic data, seismicity patterns, and satellite geodesy. Its predictive success, such as the forecasting of earthquake and volcanic hazards and the reconstruction of past continental configurations, has made it the accepted foundation of the Earth sciences.
