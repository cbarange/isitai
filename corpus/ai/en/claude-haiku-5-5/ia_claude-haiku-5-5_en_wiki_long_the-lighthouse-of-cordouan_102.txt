The Cordouan Lighthouse (French: *Phare de Cordouan*) stands on a rocky outcrop at the mouth of the Gironde estuary on France's Atlantic coast, roughly seven kilometres off the coast of Le Verdon-sur-Mer. It is widely described as the oldest lighthouse in France still in operation and is noted for its Renaissance architecture as much as for its navigational role. The site lies on a shoal that has long posed a hazard to shipping entering the estuary, and a light has been maintained there for centuries to mark the approach to the ports of Bordeaux and the surrounding region.

Earlier accounts suggest that a religious presence and some form of signal existed on the rock well before the present structure. A hermitage and chapel are reported to have occupied the site in the Middle Ages, and sailors may have relied on fires or other markers to avoid the reef. The modern tower, however, was a deliberate royal project. Construction began in 1584 under the reign of King Henry III, who wanted a substantial and permanent beacon at the estuary mouth. The work was directed by the architect Louis de Foix, who designed a tower of several stages with a decorative character unusual for a maritime structure. Because de Foix died in 1602, the building was completed under his successors, and the lighthouse was finished in 1611, during the reign of Henry IV. Accounts of the project commonly emphasise both its scale and the cost of erecting a stone tower on an exposed rock in the open Atlantic.

The lighthouse rises to approximately 67 to 68 metres, depending on the source, and its design combines a functional lantern with an ornate interior. The lower levels contain a richly decorated chamber often referred to as the royal apartment or chapel, with sculpted details and a chapel-like space reflecting the monarchy's involvement in the project. Upper floors contain the keepers' accommodation and the light mechanism. The tower's exterior has been altered over time: nineteenth-century engineers, including the architect Léonce Reynaud, carried out restoration and modification work, and the upper sections were reconstructed in a style intended to preserve the original character while improving stability and function. The building is commonly described as having around three hundred steps leading to the top.

The optical equipment of the lighthouse changed considerably over its history. Early lights relied on open fires and later on oil lamps, which were gradually improved through the eighteenth century. In the nineteenth century the installation of a Fresnel lens, based on the optical principles developed by Augustin Fresnel, greatly increased the range and reliability of the signal. Subsequent updates introduced more modern electrical lighting. The light was maintained by resident keepers for most of its existence, but the station was eventually automated, and the last keepers were withdrawn in the late twentieth century, with control transferred to remote systems administered by French maritime authorities.

The lighthouse has received formal heritage recognition. It was listed as a historic monument (*monument historique*) in the nineteenth century, and it has since been recognised internationally, being inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2005 as an outstanding example of maritime architecture of its period. Its protection reflects both its architectural importance and its role in the history of navigation on the French Atlantic coast.

Access to the lighthouse is limited by its location and by the tides. Visitors normally reach it by boat from ports such as Royan or Le Verdon, and landings depend on sea and weather conditions. Guided visits, which are organised within strict safety rules, allow tourists to see the interior and climb to the top. Conservation work is carried out periodically because the stone structure is exposed to constant wind, salt spray, and the erosive action of the sea.

The name "Cordouan" has been the subject of various explanations. One proposed origin links it to the leather known as cordovan, although the etymology is not firmly established, and sources generally present this connection as uncertain rather than proven. Regardless of its origin, the name has long been associated with the lighthouse and with the reef on which it stands. Today the Cordouan Lighthouse is regarded as a symbol of the Gironde estuary and a significant monument of French maritime heritage, admired for the combination of its Renaissance design, its long service to navigation, and its dramatic setting in the Atlantic.
