Background: Poor indoor air quality in schools is a widespread but often overlooked public health issue. Children are particularly vulnerable because their respiratory and neurological systems are still developing, they breathe more air per unit body weight than adults, and they spend a substantial fraction of their waking hours in classrooms. Exposure to indoor pollutants—including carbon dioxide, particulate matter, volatile organic compounds, and formaldehyde—has been linked to respiratory symptoms, allergic disorders, absenteeism, and reduced cognitive performance. In many regions, school buildings depend on natural ventilation through windows, which may be inadequate during heating or cooling seasons, especially in dense urban settings with elevated outdoor pollution. However, systematic data on concentrations, determinants, and health-relevant effects in schools remain limited. This study aimed to (1) characterize indoor air quality in a representative sample of primary and secondary school classrooms; (2) compare measured concentrations with international health-based guidelines; and (3) assess the short-term relationships between IAQ parameters and cognitive performance in students.

Methods: Between September 2023 and March 2024, we conducted a cross-sectional observational study in 42 classrooms from 21 schools (14 primary and 7 secondary) located in a temperate urban area. Classrooms were selected to represent a range of building ages, ventilation types, occupancy densities, and proximity to traffic. In each classroom, continuous monitoring was performed for ten school days during the heating season. Carbon dioxide, temperature, and relative humidity were logged every five minutes using calibrated non-dispersive infrared sensors. Fine particulate matter (PM2.5), inhalable particulate matter (PM10), formaldehyde, total volatile organic compounds (TVOC), and nitrogen dioxide were measured using low-cost optical and passive samplers; outdoor PM2.5 and meteorological data were obtained from the nearest fixed monitoring stations. Ventilation rates were estimated using the CO2 decay method during unoccupied periods and by mass-balance calculations during occupancy. Building characteristics, window-opening behavior, presence and operation of mechanical ventilation, cleaning products, occupancy density, and classroom activities were recorded through daily logs and technical inspections. In a subsample of 480 students aged 8–15 years, cognitive performance was assessed using standardized computer-based tasks measuring attention, working memory, and processing speed during the monitoring period. Mixed-effects regression models were used to examine determinants of pollutant concentrations and associations between IAQ parameters and cognitive outcomes, adjusting for age, sex, school, classroom, outdoor pollution, temperature, and time of day.

Results: The mean occupied CO2 concentration across all classrooms was 1285 ppm (standard deviation 395), with 68% of occupied hours exceeding 1000 ppm and 21% exceeding 1500 ppm. Peak CO2 values above 2500 ppm were observed in 14 classrooms, all without mechanical ventilation and with windows closed during cold weather. The average ventilation rate was 1.6 L/s per person (range 0.4–4.8), and 74% of classrooms did not meet the recommended minimum of 5 L/s per person. Mean PM2.5 was 15.2 µg/m³ (SD 7.8), exceeding the WHO 24-hour guideline of 15 µg/m³ in 46% of classrooms; mean PM10 was 43.1 µg/m³ (SD 18.6). Indoor PM2.5 was strongly correlated with outdoor concentrations (r = 0.71) and increased during periods of intense physical activity and with resuspension. Formaldehyde concentrations remained below health-based short-term guidelines in all classrooms (mean 21.4 µg/m³, range 5–68), while TVOC levels were elevated, with a mean of 295 µg/m³ and episodic peaks associated with cleaning, painting, and the use of scented products. Mean NO2 was 19.6 µg/m³, with higher values in schools located within 150 m of major roads. Temperature exceeded 25 °C in 29% of occupied hours, and relative humidity was below 30% in 38% of winter days, indicating dry conditions that may irritate airways and increase susceptibility to infection. Window opening during breaks reduced CO2 by 34% (95% confidence interval: 26–41) within 30 minutes but was associated with a 17% increase in indoor PM2.5 when outdoor PM2.5 exceeded 20 µg/m³. In adjusted cognitive models, each 1000 ppm increase in CO2 was associated with a 4.5% slower response time (p = 0.006) and a 2.0% lower accuracy on attention tasks; each 10 µg/m³ increase in PM2.5 was associated with a 3.3% lower working memory score (p = 0.018). No significant independent associations were found for formaldehyde or TVOC after adjustment for CO2 and particulate matter.

Conclusion: Indoor air quality in the studied schools frequently failed to meet health-based guidelines, mainly because of insufficient ventilation and, to a lesser extent, indoor sources of particles and volatile organic compounds. The observed associations between CO2, PM2.5, and modest but measurable cognitive deficits support the need for stronger regulatory standards and practical interventions in school buildings. Mechanical ventilation with heat recovery and high-efficiency particulate filtration, combined with real-time CO2 monitoring and automated alerts, would reduce exposure to indoor pollutants while maintaining thermal comfort. In existing buildings, window-opening protocols that account for outdoor air pollution, pollutant source control, and improved cleaning practices can provide interim benefits. These findings provide evidence for policymakers, school administrators, and public health agencies to prioritize indoor air quality as a modifiable determinant of children's health and learning.
