By the time the cock crowed in the osier hedge, the village of Kratov had already forgotten its own name. The discovery was not made all at once, because no one remembered that anything could be found. Bread had come out of the ovens warm and cleft along the proper seam; the wells drew up cold water; the shutters opened upon the same fields. Yet each house held a stranger with familiar hands.

Abram the clockmaker woke with a screwdriver in his fist and a woman asleep beside him. He knew the weight of the tool, the exact resistance of a brass screw, the small sigh of a mainspring when it is freed. He did not know whether the woman had come in the dark or had always been there. When she opened her eyes, she looked at him with the politeness of a traveler who has missed a stop. Neither of them screamed. There was nothing to call back and no one to call.

In the square the geese passed in a white procession, and the miller’s daughter drove them with a switch though she could not have said what they were for. Her hands knew the rhythm; her mouth knew the word *go*; between the word and the world a door had been taken off its hinges. The baker stood in his doorway holding a loaf as if it had been pressed upon him by a creditor. He broke it. The sound was correct. The smell made several people weep without knowing why, and because weeping required no explanation, they did it openly, like children.

They gathered by the well because the well rope lay there. The strongest man, or perhaps only the calmest, said, “Draw,” and someone drew. They drank in turns. Language had survived in shards. They could say *bread*, *rain*, *debt*, *God*, *enough*; these words rose and fell like birds that had lost the memory of the south but kept the habit of flying. Proper names had drained out of them. A sign above the tavern showed black letters that crawled and would not be still. A schoolchild recited the multiplication table with perfect devotion; asked what the numbers were for, she smiled and offered the stranger a crust.

Abram walked through the lanes with a photograph he had found in his own coat. The faces upon it were smooth as stones in water. He waited for pain to announce the right one and felt only the old habit of pressing his thumb beneath his ribs. In the church a man in a dark robe opened a book, and the people sighed at sounds they almost recognized. He dipped his fingers in water and touched the foreheads of those who came forward. No one could say what the water was for, yet knees bent. A newborn, delivered in the night by hands that remembered the turning of a child, was held up to the dim rafters. The midwife hushed it with a tune she had invented at that moment; the tune was accepted as ancient.

By noon they began to make rules out of what remained. The east road was called the Going-Out, and the west the Coming-In, though no one could say where either led. A child found asleep in a trough was named Bucket; a boy born under the bell was named Bell. A woman with a long scar was called River, and the name stayed because she liked the sound of it moving in other people’s mouths. They fed one another with the awkward tenderness of guests at a funeral whose dead cannot be identified.

When Abram returned to his workshop, the clocks all struck at different hours. His fingers found the faulty verge, the worn tooth, the hairline crack in an enamel face. He did not know whose life had depended on any of them. At three by the mended clock, the sound went through the square like a hand through hair. Every face lifted. Some bodies reached for absent tools; some for absent hands; some only for the wall, and held it.

Evening came without permission. Fires were lit by habit, couples formed by nearness and appetite, and the newborn passed from lap to lap. Each woman crooned a different promise. No one asked after fathers or mothers; there were only older bodies with the same bewildered eyes, and younger ones already learning to be careful around beauty.

At dusk a word rose under the tongue of the village: *return*. None could say whence or to whom. They practiced it softly, as if it were a prayer in a language dead before the snow. *Return*, they said to the locked houses, to the dark fields, to the stranger warming herself at their fire. *Return.* And the wind, which had kept every sound it had ever stolen, stood still among the chimneys, almost kind, almost ready.
