Background: Agricultural intensification is widely recognized as a driver of biodiversity loss, but impacts on soil organisms remain less understood than for aboveground taxa. Soil biodiversity underpins critical ecosystem functions including nutrient cycling, organic matter decomposition, water regulation, and natural pest suppression. We assessed how contrasting management regimes affect multitrophic diversity in agricultural soils across temperate cropping systems.

Methods: We sampled 72 fields under conventional, reduced-till, and organic management across three regions during two growing seasons. Soil bacteria, fungi, protists, nematodes, and microarthropods were characterized using amplicon sequencing and morphological identification. Soil physicochemical properties, management history, and landscape context were recorded. Alpha and beta diversity, co-occurrence networks, and functional guild composition were analyzed using mixed-effects models and multivariate ordination.

Results: Organic and reduced-till systems supported 28-42% higher taxonomic richness and greater functional diversity than conventionally tilled fields, particularly for mycorrhizal fungi, predatory nematodes, and decomposer microarthropods. Intensive nitrogen fertilization and pesticide use were negatively associated with network complexity and specialist taxa. Crop rotation diversity positively predicted microbial evenness and soil organic carbon. Community composition diverged strongly by tillage intensity and soil pH, explaining 38% of variation.

Conclusion: Diversified, low-disturbance management enhances biodiversity across multiple soil trophic levels and strengthens functional networks linked to soil health. Integrating reduced tillage, organic amendments, and diverse rotations can reconcile agricultural productivity with conservation of soil biodiversity and associated ecosystem services.
