Farmers in the Guadalquivir valley in Andalusia have begun leaving fields fallow weeks earlier than usual, as southern Europe confronts another year of severe water scarcity after below-average autumn and winter rainfall across much of Spain, Italy, Portugal and Greece.

Reservoir levels in the hardest-hit basins in southern and north-eastern Spain remain well below the seasonal average, according to data from the Spanish environment ministry. In Andalusia and Catalonia, some reservoirs are reported at around a third of capacity, prompting regional governments to maintain emergency restrictions on agricultural and urban water use that were first introduced during the prolonged drought of 2023 and 2024.

In Italy, attention is again focused on the Po valley, the country’s largest agricultural plain, and on the islands of Sicily and Sardinia. Farmers’ associations have warned of reduced flows in the Po and its tributaries after a dry winter in the Alps left snowpack below normal. In Sicily, where drought conditions have persisted for more than a year, livestock farmers and growers of wheat, olives and citrus fruit say wells and artificial lakes have not recovered, despite heavy storms in other parts of the country.

Portugal and Greece are facing similar pressures. In Portugal’s Alentejo and Algarve regions, where tourism increases summer demand, municipalities have imposed night-time restrictions on irrigation of parks and gardens, car washing and filling of swimming pools. In Greece, officials in Attica and on Aegean islands including Crete, Naxos and Rhodes have reported falling groundwater levels and increased reliance on desalination and water transported by tanker to supplement local supplies.

The European Commission’s Joint Research Centre and the EU’s Copernicus Climate Change Service have recorded soil moisture deficits across large parts of the Mediterranean basin through the winter months. Scientists say the pattern is consistent with longer-term trends: higher average temperatures increasing evaporation, more erratic rainfall concentrated in intense downpours, and shorter, less reliable wet seasons.

For agriculture, the consequences are already visible. Olive oil production in Spain, the world’s largest producer, has been volatile in recent seasons after heat and water stress during flowering. Cereal growers in Spain and Italy say yields are likely to be reduced again if spring rainfall does not materialise. Fruit and vegetable producers in Catalonia, Valencia and the Algarve, who depend heavily on irrigation, face quotas on water allocations.

“It is not just one dry year, it is the accumulation,” said a hydrologist at the University of Barcelona. “When reservoirs do not refill in autumn and winter, farmers start the growing season with a deficit they cannot recover. Higher temperatures mean crops and soils need more water even when rainfall is the same as twenty years ago.”

Urban water managers say the emphasis has shifted from short-term emergency measures to permanent reductions in consumption. Barcelona, which relied on desalination plants and water shipped by tanker during the worst months of the 2023-2024 drought, continues to limit water use per resident and to recycle treated wastewater for street cleaning and irrigation of parks. Similar measures are in place in Seville, Malaga and parts of Sicily, where tanker deliveries remain common in hilltop towns.

Tourism, a vital source of income for much of southern Europe, has also been affected. Hotel owners in the Balearics, the Costa Brava and the Algarve have installed water-saving systems, urged guests to reuse towels and limited the filling of pools. Local authorities insist visitors remain welcome but say enforcement of hosepipe bans and fines for excessive use will continue through the summer.

Energy production has added to concerns. Hydropower output in Spain, Portugal and Italy, which normally provides an important share of electricity in wet months, has been below average in dry periods, increasing reliance on solar power and other sources. Grid operators say the rapid expansion of solar capacity in Spain and Greece has helped compensate, but warn that prolonged drought complicates management of both electricity and water systems.

In Brussels, officials say drought is now treated as a structural risk rather than an exceptional event. EU funds for rural development and cohesion policy have been directed towards modernising irrigation networks, repairing leaking urban pipes — which in some southern cities lose more than a fifth of treated water — expanding desalination, and improving wastewater reuse. The Commission has urged member states to accelerate river basin management plans and to coordinate withdrawals from shared rivers such as the Tagus, Douro and Guadiana.

Climate researchers caution that adaptation alone will not prevent more frequent shortages. The Mediterranean is identified by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change as a climate change hotspot, warming faster than the global average, with projections showing a decline in summer precipitation and longer dry spells.

For now, much depends on spring rain. Meteorologists in Spain and Italy say rainfall in March and April is critical for replenishing soils before the heat of summer. Without it, regional governments are expected to tighten restrictions, with priority given by law to drinking water over agriculture and industry.

In Cordoba province, where wheat fields are already showing signs of stress, one farmer said the calculation was becoming harder each year. “My father worried about prices. We worry about whether there will be water to finish the season,” he said. “Every year we plant a little less, invest a little less, because we do not know.”
