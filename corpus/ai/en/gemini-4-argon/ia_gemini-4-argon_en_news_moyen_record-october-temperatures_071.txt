Unseasonably warm air has swept across the country this week, pushing October temperatures to historic highs and shattering records that have stood for more than half a century. 

The National Meteorological Service confirmed on Thursday that the average national temperature reached 26.4 degrees Celsius yesterday, surpassing the previous mid-October record set in 1968 by nearly two degrees. In several southern and central regions, the mercury climbed past 30 degrees Celsius, levels more typical of late July than mid-autumn. 

Meteorologists attribute the anomalous warmth to a persistent high-pressure system, often referred to as a heat dome, stalled over the continent. This blockage in the jet stream has drawn a steady plume of subtropical air northward while preventing cooler Atlantic fronts from moving inland. 

Climatologists warn that while localized weather patterns are the immediate cause, the intensity of the heat carries the unmistakable fingerprint of broader climate change. Data from the national weather archive shows that five of the warmest Octobers on record have occurred within the past decade.

The extended summer conditions have had immediate effects on agriculture and infrastructure. Farmers in the central valleys report that the lack of autumn rainfall and high evaporation rates are delaying the planting of winter cereal crops. Meanwhile, municipal water authorities in three southern provinces have extended summer usage restrictions for an additional two weeks as reservoir levels remain critically low.

Conversely, the energy sector has seen a sharp delay in the seasonal uptick of heating demand, providing a temporary buffer for national gas reserves ahead of winter. 

Forecasters expect the high-pressure ridge to break down by late Sunday. A cold front approaching from the northwest is projected to bring scattered showers and drop temperatures back toward seasonal averages early next week.
