The immune system is a complex biological network of organs, cells, and proteins that defends an organism against disease. According to immunologist Charles Janeway, its primary biological function is to identify and eliminate a wide variety of exogenous pathogens, ranging from viruses and bacteria to parasitic worms, while simultaneously distinguishing them from the organism's own healthy tissue. This critical mechanism of self-recognition versus non-self-recognition was initially theorized by Sir Frank Macfarlane Burnet in the mid-twentieth century. In vertebrates, the defensive network is fundamentally divided into two major evolutionary subsystems: the innate immune system and the adaptive immune system. Both components collaborate closely through biochemical signaling to ensure comprehensive physiological protection and cellular homeostasis.

The innate immune system provides an immediate, non-specific defense against invading microorganisms. Found in nearly all forms of life, it utilizes physical barriers like the skin alongside specialized leukocytes, such as phagocytes and natural killer cells, to induce localized inflammation and destroy foreign entities. Conversely, the adaptive immune system, which evolved specifically in early jawed vertebrates, delivers a highly targeted biological response. As detailed in standard immunological literature, such as the texts of Kenneth Murphy, the adaptive system relies heavily on B and T lymphocytes to recognize specific molecular structures known as antigens. This process generates immunological memory, allowing the host organism to mount faster and considerably stronger defensive responses upon subsequent encounters with the same pathogen, a physiological principle that forms the scientific basis of modern vaccination. However, dysfunctions within these intricate defense networks can cause severe clinical pathologies. Immunodeficiency occurs when the system is notably less active than normal, leading to recurring, life-threatening infections. Conversely, autoimmunity results from a hyperactive system attacking normal host tissues, driving chronic medical conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis and type 1 diabetes.
