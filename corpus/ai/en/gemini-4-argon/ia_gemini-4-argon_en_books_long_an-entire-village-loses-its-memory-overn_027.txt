The morning did not arrive so much as it simply existed, pale and unanchored, over the slate roofs of Oakhaven. When the mist finally lifted from the cobblestone square, it revealed a population of ghosts who had not yet died. They stood in their doorways, shivering in linen nightshirts and woollen shawls, blinking at the sunlight as though it were a foreign currency they did not know how to spend.

In the night, some thief of the mind had crept through the valley, unspooling the threads of every life and leaving only the blank spindles behind.

A tall man with flour ingrained in the creases of his knuckles stood before a brick oven that still radiated yesterday’s warmth. He looked at the wooden peel in his hand, then at the loaves rising in their tin troughs. He did not know his name, nor did he know the word for bread, though his stomach contracted at the dense, yeasty perfume of it. Across the lane, a woman leaned from a second-story casement. Her fingers absently traced a silver locket at her throat. When she pried it open with a thumbnail, she found two painted miniatures inside—a smiling boy and a stern, bearded man. She stared at their tiny, brushstroke eyes with a profound and hollow curiosity, wondering to whom these miniature captives belonged, and why the sight of them made her ribs ache as though pressed by iron bands.

One by one, they drifted into the square. There was no panic, for panic requires the knowledge of how things ought to be, and the villagers had been stripped of all comparison. Instead, there was a vast, somnambulistic quiet.

They moved among one another like careful beasts in a strange wood. An elderly man with a brass pocket watch ticking against his ribs approached the flour-stained man. They did not speak; language remained intact in their throats, a tool waiting to be used, yet neither knew what question to ask first. To ask *Who are you?* seemed foolish when the question *Who am I?* remained a smooth, impenetrable wall.

Around them, the village went on testifying to lives they could no longer claim. A yellow hound trotted into the square, its tail flagging with uncertain joy. It approached a young girl in a mud-hemmed dress, pressing its damp nose into her palm. The girl looked down at the creature. Her hand rose, almost of its own volition, to scratch the rough fur behind its left ear—the exact spot where an old tick scar lay hidden. The dog whimpered in recognition, yet the girl’s eyes remained wide and vacant, marveling at the mechanics of her own fingers, which seemed to possess a wisdom entirely divorced from her mind.

It was the objects that bore the weight of their vanished history. The cobblestones were grooved by the iron rims of carts none of them remembered driving. On a wooden bench outside the tavern, someone had left a half-whittled piece of ash wood and a folding knife. A man with a scarred thumb picked it up. The handle settled into his calluses like water filling a basin. He pressed the blade to the wood and shaved off a curl of pale timber, watching it fall to his boots, bewildered by the sudden, orphan competence of his hands.

By midday, the church bell did not ring, because the man whose duty it was to pull the hemp rope was sitting by the stone fountain, fascinated by the reflection of his own grey beard in the water.

Slowly, driven by a chill that had nothing to do with the autumn air, the villagers began to gather in the center of the market. They looked at one another’s faces, searching for a tether. A man with greying temples found himself standing beside a woman whose hair was braided with the same blue ribbon that tied the curtains of the cottage he had woken in. He looked at the silver band on her fourth finger, and then at the matching indentation of pale skin on his own.

"I think," the woman whispered, her voice rusted from disuse, "that I have looked at you for a very long time."

The man studied the network of fine lines around her eyes. They were deep, carved by decades of squinting against the sun or laughing at jokes now evaporated into the ether. He did not know her virtue, her temper, or the sound of her weeping. He did not know if he had loved her fiercely or merely endured her presence across a cold hearth.

"Yes," he replied, reaching out to touch the blue ribbon, his fingers trembling. "Your face is tired in the same way mine feels."

Around them, others were beginning to speak, their voices low and tentative, like pebbles dropped into a deep well. They did not try to excavate the past; there were no spades sharp enough to pierce the fog that had settled behind their brows. Instead, the man with the flour on his hands went back inside, drew a warm, slightly burned loaf from the dying oven, and carried it out to the cobblestones. He broke it in half and offered a piece to the woman with the silver locket. She took it, her eyes watering with a gratitude she could not name, and as they ate in the pale light of the unremembered afternoon, the village began the slow, terrifying work of inventing itself from the ashes of the morning.
