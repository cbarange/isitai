Background: 
Early diagnosis remains the cornerstone of effective therapeutic intervention across a multitude of life-threatening pathologies, particularly in oncology, neurology, and cardiology. When pathologies are identified in their nascent stages, patient survival rates improve dramatically, treatment morbidity is minimized, and healthcare costs are substantially reduced. Historically, medical imaging modalities—including Computed Tomography (CT), Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), and Positron Emission Tomography (PET)—have served as primary non-invasive tools for disease detection. However, conventional diagnostic paradigms rely heavily on visual assessment by human radiologists. This approach is inherently constrained by the resolution limits of the human eye, inter-observer variability, diagnostic fatigue, and the overarching reality that macroscopic structural anomalies often manifest only after significant molecular and cellular progression has occurred. Consequently, subtle, pre-symptomatic tissue alterations frequently go undetected during routine screenings, leading to delayed diagnoses and poorer clinical outcomes. In recent years, the convergence of high-resolution multimodal imaging, quantitative radiomics, and advanced artificial intelligence (AI), specifically deep learning architectures, has presented an unprecedented opportunity to overcome these limitations. By extracting high-dimensional, sub-visual features from standard digital medical images, computational models can potentially identify micro-architectural changes indicative of early-stage disease long before they become clinically apparent to human interpreters. Despite promising preliminary studies, the clinical translation of these technologies has been hindered by small, single-center datasets, lack of algorithmic generalizability, and single-modality limitations. Therefore, this study aimed to develop, validate, and clinically evaluate a novel, multimodal deep learning radiomic framework designed to enhance the early, pre-symptomatic detection of solid tumors and neurodegenerative biomarkers across a large, diverse, multi-institutional cohort.

Methods: 
A retrospective and prospective, multicenter cohort study was conducted utilizing anonymized imaging data collected from twelve tertiary healthcare institutions between January 2018 and December 2023. The dataset comprised 48,500 patients who underwent routine screening or diagnostic workups, encompassing a total of 145,000 multimodal imaging scans, including low-dose thoracic CT, multiparametric MRI (mpMRI), and 18F-FDG PET/CT. The cohort was longitudinally tracked, with ground truth established via subsequent histopathological confirmation for oncological cases (specifically early-stage non-small cell lung cancer and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma) or definitive longitudinal clinical progression for neurodegenerative cases (specifically prodromal Alzheimer’s disease). The dataset was partitioned into a training set (70%), an internal validation set (15%), and an external, geographically distinct test set (15%) to rigorously assess generalizability. All images underwent standardized preprocessing, including isotropic voxel resampling, skull stripping or organ segmentation via a pre-trained 3D U-Net, and intensity normalization to mitigate inter-scanner variability. We engineered a hybrid computational architecture, termed "Rad-VisionNet," which integrates a 3D Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) with a Vision Transformer (ViT) backbone to simultaneously capture localized textural micro-features and global spatial context across fused imaging modalities. Additionally, 1,240 standardized quantitative radiomic features (spanning first-order statistics, shape descriptors, and gray-level co-occurrence matrices) were extracted and integrated into the network’s fully connected layers alongside baseline demographic data. Model performance was evaluated using the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC-ROC), sensitivity, specificity, and Positive Predictive Value (PPV). To establish clinical utility, a blinded reader study was conducted wherein a panel of ten board-certified radiologists (mean experience: 11.4 years) independently evaluated the external test set both with and without the assistance of the Rad-VisionNet decision-support overlay, which utilized Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) to provide explainable visual heatmaps of high-risk anatomical regions.

Results: 
In the external test cohort (n = 7,275 patients), the Rad-VisionNet framework demonstrated exceptional diagnostic accuracy across all targeted pathologies, significantly outperforming both single-modality baseline models and unassisted human readers. For the detection of sub-centimeter, Stage IA non-small cell lung cancer on low-dose CT, the model achieved an AUC-ROC of 0.964 (95% Confidence Interval [CI]: 0.951–0.977), with a sensitivity of 94.2% and a specificity of 91.8%. In the notoriously challenging domain of early-stage pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma via mpMRI and CT fusion, the algorithm yielded an AUC-ROC of 0.938 (95% CI: 0.919–0.957), successfully identifying occult lesions on scans acquired an average of 14.2 months (interquartile range: 8.5–19.6 months) prior to standard clinical diagnosis. For prodromal Alzheimer’s disease, multimodal analysis of structural MRI and PET data predicted mild cognitive impairment-to-dementia conversion within 36 months with an accuracy of 91.5% (AUC-ROC: 0.949, 95% CI: 0.932–0.966). Comparatively, the standalone radiologist panel achieved a pooled sensitivity of 76.4% and a specificity of 88.2% across all tasks (p < 0.0001). Notably, the false-positive rate of the AI framework was reduced by 38.5% compared to traditional computer-aided detection (CAD) systems, largely attributable to the cross-modality attention mechanisms of the Vision Transformer, which effectively filtered out benign calcifications and motion artifacts. When radiologists utilized the Rad-VisionNet Grad-CAM heatmaps as a concurrent reading aid, their pooled diagnostic sensitivity increased significantly from 76.4% to 92.8% (p < 0.001), while inter-observer agreement (measured via Fleiss’ kappa) improved from moderate (κ = 0.58) to near-perfect (κ = 0.86). Furthermore, average interpretation time per complex multimodal study decreased by 31.4% (from 14.2 minutes to 9.7 minutes, p < 0.01), demonstrating substantial workflow efficiency gains. 

Conclusion: 
The integration of deep learning-based radiomics with multimodal medical imaging represents a paradigm shift in the early, pre-symptomatic diagnosis of complex diseases. The proposed Rad-VisionNet architecture successfully decodes sub-visual, quantitative imaging biomarkers, enabling the identification of oncological and neurodegenerative pathologies more than a year prior to conventional diagnostic thresholds. Crucially, the study demonstrates that the optimal deployment of this technology lies in a synergistic human-AI collaboration; providing radiologists with explainable, localized probability maps significantly elevates diagnostic sensitivity, reduces inter-reader variability, and accelerates clinical workflows without inflating false-positive rates. By shifting the diagnostic timeline backward, this non-invasive computational approach holds the potential to transform clinical management, allowing for the initiation of targeted therapies at a stage when disease burden is minimal and potentially reversible. Future prospective, randomized clinical trials are warranted to evaluate the long-term impact of AI-augmented early imaging diagnosis on overall patient survival, quality of life, and health-economic outcomes in diverse global healthcare settings.
