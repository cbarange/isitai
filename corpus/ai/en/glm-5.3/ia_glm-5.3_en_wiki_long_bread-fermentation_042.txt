Bread fermentation is the biological process by which microorganisms, primarily yeasts and lactic acid bacteria, convert carbohydrates in dough into carbon dioxide, ethanol, and organic acids. The process serves two principal functions in breadmaking: the carbon dioxide produced leavens the dough by expanding gas cells trapped within its gluten network, and the metabolic by-products contribute flavor, aroma, and changes in texture and keeping quality. Fermentation is among the oldest applications of biotechnology known to humanity and remains central to both artisanal and industrial bread production.

Archaeological and textual evidence indicates that leavened bread was produced in ancient Egypt by approximately the third millennium BCE, likely after dough left standing was observed to rise and acquire a lighter texture. The practice spread throughout the Mediterranean and Near Eastern worlds. The Roman naturalist Pliny the Elder recorded in the first century CE that peoples of Gaul and Iberia used the foam skimmed from beer as a leavening agent, while bakers without access to beer employed grape residue. Scientific understanding of the process, however, remained limited until the nineteenth century. Louis Pasteur's experiments in the 1850s and 1860s established that fermentation is caused by living microorganisms rather than by purely chemical action, identifying yeast as the responsible agent. This insight enabled the controlled production of baker's yeast, and by the late nineteenth century compressed yeast had largely displaced sourdough and barm starters in commercial bakeries in Europe and North America.

The dominant yeast in most yeasted breads is Saccharomyces cerevisiae, a species selected for its rapid gas production and tolerance of bread dough conditions. Its metabolism depends on enzymes present in flour and in the yeast cells themselves: cereal amylases break down damaged starch into maltose and smaller sugars, which the yeast then ferments, producing ethanol and carbon dioxide through the glycolytic pathway. The gluten proteins in wheat dough, and corresponding proteins in rye, provide the viscoelastic matrix that retains the gas, allowing the dough to expand. Dough strength, hydration, and mixing thus interact with fermentation rate to determine loaf volume and crumb structure.

Sourdough fermentation involves a mixed culture of lactic acid bacteria and yeasts, often including species such as Lactobacillus sanfranciscensis and Kazachstania humilis, maintained in a flour-and-water starter propagated across batches. Cereal scientists have described a quasi-symbiotic relationship in these ecosystems: the bacteria produce acids that acidify the dough and favor acid-tolerant yeasts, while the yeasts ferment sugars the bacteria cannot metabolize efficiently. Heterofermentative lactic acid bacteria generate lactic acid, acetic acid, carbon dioxide, and various volatile compounds. The ratio of lactic to acetic acid, which strongly influences flavor, is governed by fermentation temperature, hydration, and the availability of specific substrates. Sourdough processes are typically longer than yeasted ones, often requiring many hours or multiple stages, and the resulting acidity inhibits mold and rope-forming bacteria, historically extending shelf life before chemical preservatives were available.

Numerous process variables affect fermentation outcomes. Temperature is among the most significant, with yeast activity roughly doubling for each interval of several degrees Celsius within its viable range; bakers accordingly retard fermentation by refrigerating dough to extend flavor development. Salt slows yeast activity and strengthens gluten, while high sugar concentrations can osmotically inhibit less tolerant strains. Pre-ferments such as poolish, biga, and sponge methods, in which a portion of the total flour and water is fermented in advance, are used to build flavor and dough maturity before final mixing. Industrial practice spans a wide spectrum: the Chorleywood Bread Process, developed in Britain in 1961, mechanically develops dough and permits a single short fermentation, while sponge-and-dough systems retain longer bulk fermentation for flavor. The relative emphasis on speed versus flavor development continues to distinguish mass-produced and craft bread traditions.

Beyond leavening, fermentation substantially shapes bread's sensory and nutritional properties. Food chemists have identified dozens of flavor-active compounds in fermented dough, including alcohols, esters, aldehydes, and diacetyl, and sourdough acids contribute characteristic tang and enhance perceived wheat aroma. Fermentation also affects crust color, since residual reducing sugars participate in Maillard browning, and modifies crumb softness and staling behavior. Nutrition researchers have reported that sourdough fermentation degrades phytic acid through microbial and cereal phytases, improving the bioavailability of minerals such as zinc and iron, and controlled studies have found lower glycemic responses to sourdough breads than to otherwise similar yeasted breads, attributed in part to acid-mediated changes in starch digestibility. Some studies additionally suggest partial degradation of gluten proteins during long fermentations, though findings regarding effects on gluten sensitivity remain debated in the scientific literature.

In contemporary practice, bread fermentation encompasses both traditional methods, which have attracted renewed consumer and research interest, and highly optimized industrial systems using selected strains, enzymatic supplements, and controlled proofing environments. Its study draws on microbiology, cereal chemistry, and food engineering, and it is generally regarded by historians of technology as one of the longest-continuously practiced examples of humans managing microbial processes for food production.
