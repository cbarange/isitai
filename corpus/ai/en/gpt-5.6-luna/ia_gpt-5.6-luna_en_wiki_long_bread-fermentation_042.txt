Bread fermentation is the biological process in which microorganisms transform components of dough, producing gases and metabolic compounds that influence loaf volume, texture, aroma, and flavor. In most conventional bread, the principal organism is baker’s yeast, *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*. In sourdough, yeasts coexist with lactic acid bacteria, which generate organic acids in addition to carbon dioxide. Fermentation generally occurs after flour, water, salt, and leavening agents have been mixed, although some methods use preferments or sourdough cultures that begin fermenting hours or days earlier (Cauvain and Young, *Technology of Breadmaking*, 2007).

The central function of baker’s yeast is the conversion of fermentable sugars into carbon dioxide and ethanol. A simplified representation is glucose → carbon dioxide + ethanol + energy. The carbon dioxide dissolves initially in the dough’s water phase and then forms or enlarges gas cells. These cells are retained by the gluten network developed from wheat proteins, particularly gliadin and glutenin. During baking, the gas expands as its temperature rises, while water vapor and additional carbon dioxide contribute to “oven spring,” the rapid expansion that occurs before the dough structure sets. Most of the ethanol and a substantial portion of the carbon dioxide escape during baking, although yeast fermentation products and secondary metabolites contribute to the bread’s aroma (Cauvain and Young, 2007).

Flour contains relatively little free sugar, but it contains starch, which is broken down during fermentation by enzymes. Flour’s amylases convert starch into smaller dextrins and sugars, including maltose, while yeast and other microorganisms use sugars such as glucose, fructose, and maltose. The availability of these sugars depends on flour composition, enzyme activity, dough temperature, and fermentation time. Damaged starch granules generally provide more accessible substrates than intact granules. Commercial bread formulas may also include sugar or other ingredients that alter yeast activity, browning, and flavor.

Fermentation is closely connected with dough development. Mixing hydrates flour proteins and permits gluten to form, while mechanical work aligns and strengthens the developing network. Fermentation then causes the dough to expand and modifies its physical properties through continued enzymatic activity, acidification, and the redistribution of water. During bulk fermentation, the dough may be folded or punched down to redistribute yeast, temperature, and nutrients and to regulate gas-cell structure. A later proof, or final fermentation, takes place after shaping and before baking. The degree of fermentation is a major determinant of loaf volume: under-fermented dough may have a dense crumb, whereas over-fermented dough can lose strength and collapse because its gluten network can no longer retain the accumulated gas.

Temperature is one of the principal controls on fermentation. Within a suitable range, warmer dough generally ferments faster, while cooler dough ferments more slowly and may develop different flavor compounds. Excessive heat can damage yeast or accelerate fermentation beyond the dough’s structural capacity. Salt usually slows yeast activity and strengthens gluten, while high concentrations of sugar or fat can also reduce the rate at which yeast obtains water and metabolizes sugars. Hydration, dough acidity, yeast quantity, flour type, and fermentation duration interact, so no single fermentation temperature or time is appropriate for all breads.

Sourdough fermentation involves a stable community of lactic acid bacteria and yeasts maintained in a mixture of flour and water. Common bacterial groups include species of *Lactobacillus* and related genera, although the microbial composition varies with flour, environment, refreshments, temperature, and hydration. Lactic acid bacteria produce lactic acid and, in many cultures, acetic acid and other compounds. These acids lower dough pH, producing the characteristic sour taste and affecting gluten behavior, enzyme activity, mineral availability, and microbial competition. Some bacteria are homofermentative, producing mainly lactic acid, whereas heterofermentative species produce lactic acid together with carbon dioxide, ethanol or acetic acid, and other metabolites (Hammes et al., “Microbial Ecology of Cereal Fermentations,” *Trends in Food Science & Technology*, 2005).

Sourdough yeasts may include *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, *Kazachstania humilis*, and other species. Yeasts and bacteria interact by competing for nutrients and by producing compounds that one another can tolerate or use. Repeated refreshments select for communities adapted to the particular flour and maintenance conditions. Sourdough fermentation can improve flavor complexity and may contribute to delayed staling through changes in acidity and crumb structure, although outcomes depend strongly on formulation and process. It does not automatically make bread nutritionally superior, and claims concerning digestibility or reduced glycemic response vary with the bread’s ingredients and fermentation conditions (De Vuyst et al., “Sourdough Fermentation Processes,” *Trends in Food Science & Technology*, 2016).

Fermentation also affects food safety and shelf life. Acidification can inhibit some spoilage organisms, while fermentation by-products influence the growth of molds and rope-forming bacteria. Nevertheless, fermentation is not a substitute for hygienic handling or adequate baking. The history of bread includes both spontaneous sourdough fermentation and the later use of pressed or dried commercial yeast, whose standardized activity made production more predictable. Modern bakeries employ direct dough processes, sponge-and-dough systems, poolishes, bigas, and sourdoughs to control fermentation rate and flavor. Across these methods, fermentation remains a balance between microbial metabolism and the physical capacity of dough to retain gas and withstand baking (Cauvain and Young, 2007; De Vuyst et al., 2016).
