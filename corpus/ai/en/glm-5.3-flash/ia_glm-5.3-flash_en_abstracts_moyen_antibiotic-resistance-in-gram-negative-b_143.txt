**Background:** The rapid emergence of antibiotic resistance in Gram-negative bacteria, particularly extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Enterobacterales and carbapenem-resistant strains, represents a major threat to public health. Understanding the mechanisms and epidemiological trends of resistance is essential to guide antimicrobial stewardship and infection control policies.

**Methods:** A prospective surveillance study was conducted over 24 months in three tertiary care hospitals. Clinical isolates of Gram-negative bacilli (n = 2,847) were collected from blood, urine, and respiratory specimens. Antimicrobial susceptibility was determined by broth microdilution according to CLSI guidelines. Resistance genes (blaESBL, blaKPC, blaNDM, mcr-1) were detected by multiplex PCR and whole-genome sequencing. Risk factors for multidrug-resistant (MDR) carriage were analyzed using multivariable logistic regression.

**Results:** Overall, 34.2% of isolates were ESBL producers and 6.8% were carbapenem-resistant. blaCTX-M-15 was the predominant ESBL gene (72% of ESBL producers), while blaNDM-1 accounted for 58% of carbapenemase genes. Colistin resistance mediated by mcr-1 was identified in 1.1% of isolates. MDR carriage was significantly associated with prior antibiotic exposure (OR = 3.4; 95% CI: 2.1–5.5), recent hospitalization (OR = 2.8; 95% CI: 1.7–4.6), and indwelling devices (OR = 2.2; 95% CI: 1.4–3.5). Mortality among patients infected with carbapenem-resistant strains reached 31%, compared with 12% for susceptible strains (p < 0.001).

**Conclusion:** Resistance in Gram-negative bacteria is high and driven mainly by ESBL and carbapenemase genes, with prior antibiotic exposure as the key modifiable risk factor. These findings underscore the urgent need for strengthened stewardship programs, rapid molecular diagnostics, and enhanced surveillance to limit the dissemination of MDR Gram-negative pathogens.
