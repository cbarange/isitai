**Background:** The widespread adoption of remote work since the COVID-19 pandemic has fundamentally altered commuting patterns, yet its long-term effects on urban mobility remain insufficiently quantified. This study examines how varying intensities of teleworking influence daily travel behavior, modal choice, and peak-hour congestion in metropolitan areas.

**Methods:** We analyzed mobility data from four European metropolitan regions (Paris, Lyon, Amsterdam, and Barcelona) collected between 2019 and 2024. The dataset combined mobile network operator data, public transit smart-card records, and traffic counts, alongside a longitudinal survey of 12,400 workers categorized by telework intensity (none, 1–2 days, 3+ days per week). Difference-in-differences models estimated changes in trip frequency, distance, and mode share, controlling for income, household composition, and urban density.

**Results:** Workers telecommuting 1–2 days per week reduced total weekly trips by 18%, while those teleworking 3+ days reduced trips by 41%, though partially offset by a 23% increase in non-commuting trips (leisure, shopping), often over longer distances and by car. Public transit ridership among hybrid workers declined by 27% on telework days but showed only partial recovery on office days, contributing to a net 12% transit revenue loss. Car mode share for remaining commutes increased by 9 percentage points among hybrid workers. Peak-hour congestion decreased by 15% overall, but midday congestion rose by 8%, flattening the traditional demand curve.

**Conclusion:** Remote work substantially reduces commuting but induces compensatory travel and modal shifts toward private vehicles, attenuating expected environmental benefits. Urban transport policies should adapt pricing, service frequency, and network design to temporally redistributed demand rather than assuming uniform ridership declines.
