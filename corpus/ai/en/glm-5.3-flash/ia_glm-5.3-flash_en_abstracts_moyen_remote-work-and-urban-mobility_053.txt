Background: The widespread adoption of remote work following the COVID-19 pandemic has fundamentally altered commuting patterns, yet its long-term effects on urban mobility remain insufficiently quantified. Understanding these shifts is critical for transportation planning, infrastructure investment, and climate policy.

Methods: We analyzed anonymized mobile device location data, transit smartcard records, and traffic sensor measurements from six metropolitan areas (population 1–5 million) across the 2019–2024 period. Difference-in-differences models compared travel behavior between high-remote-work and low-remote-work sectors, controlling for seasonality, fuel prices, and demographic shifts. Supplementary survey data (n = 12,400) captured stated preferences regarding hybrid schedules and mode choice.

Results: Average weekday commuting trips declined by 23% relative to 2019 baselines, while non-commute trips (errands, leisure) increased by 11%, partially offsetting reductions in total vehicle-kilometers traveled (net reduction of 9%). Peak-hour congestion decreased by 17%, with pronounced flattening of morning peaks, but midday congestion rose by 6% in suburban commercial corridors. Public transit ridership recovered to only 74% of pre-pandemic levels, disproportionately affecting commuter rail (-31%) versus bus services (-12%). Heterogeneity analysis revealed stronger effects in knowledge-economy districts and among households beyond 15 km from urban cores. Survey results indicated 62% of hybrid workers consolidated trips around office days.

Conclusion: Remote work has produced durable, structurally significant changes in urban mobility characterized by temporal redistribution rather than uniform trip reduction. Transit systems require service restructuring toward off-peak and dispersed origins-destinations, while modest net emission gains may be eroded by induced suburban travel. Policymakers should plan for persistent hybrid work patterns rather than assuming full reversion to pre-pandemic commuting.
