The Cordouan lighthouse is an active lighthouse located at the mouth of the Gironde estuary in southwestern France, approximately seven kilometres offshore from the coast near Royan and Le Verdon-sur-Mer. Often described as the "Versailles of the sea," it is the oldest lighthouse in France still in operation and is frequently cited among the oldest working lighthouses in the world.

The structure has origins in the 14th century, when a first tower was erected on the rocky Cordouan plateau, traditionally attributed to the "Black Prince," Edward of Wales, around 1360. The current edifice was largely built between 1584 and 1611 under the direction of the architect Louis de Foix, who transformed it into an elaborate monument combining defensive, utilitarian, and palatial features, including an apartments level with chapel and royal chambers. The tower was substantially raised in the 18th century, reaching its present height of approximately 68 metres, with the engineer Joseph Teulère responsible for the upper section in 1724–1729.

The lighthouse was long administered by the maritime authorities of Bordeaux and remains maintained by the French state. It was automated in 2006, though lighthouse keepers continued to be stationed there for some time, making it the last manned lighthouse on the French coast. Its lantern, historically fuelled successively by wood, coal, oil, and finally electricity, remains a significant aid to navigation for shipping entering the Gironde toward Bordeaux.

In 2021, Cordouan lighthouse was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in recognition of its architectural and historical significance as a masterpiece of Renaissance engineering and a monument to the history of maritime signalling.
