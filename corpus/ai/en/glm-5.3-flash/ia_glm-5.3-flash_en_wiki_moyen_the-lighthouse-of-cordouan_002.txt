The Cordouan lighthouse is an active lighthouse located at the mouth of the Gironde estuary in southwestern France, near the town of Le Verdon-sur-Mer. Often called the "Versailles of the sea," it is the oldest lighthouse in France still in operation. Built between 1584 and 1611 on the Cordouan plateau, a rocky reef exposed at low tide, it was designed by the engineer Louis de Foix under commission from King Henry III and completed under Henry IV.

The structure combines Renaissance architectural ambition with maritime function, featuring a chapel dedicated to King Louis IX (Saint Louis), royal apartments, and an apothecary's storeroom. Its original light source consisted of burning wood and later coal; over the centuries it underwent successive modifications, notably by Joseph Teulère in the late 18th century, who raised the tower, and by engineers in the 19th century who installed rotating Fresnel lens systems, then a technological advance developed in France.

Standing approximately 68 meters tall, the lighthouse comprises several levels accessible by internal staircases, culminating in a lantern room and an open platform offering panoramic views of the estuary. It has been guarded continuously since its construction, a distinction that contributed to its cultural significance.

In 2021, Cordouan lighthouse was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as "Cordouan Lighthouse," recognized as an outstanding example of the art of lighthouse construction, illustrating the history of signal lights and bearing exceptional testimony to maritime heritage. It remains a major landmark for navigation into the Gironde estuary, guiding vessels toward Bordeaux, and is open to visitors during favorable sea conditions, reachable only by boat.
