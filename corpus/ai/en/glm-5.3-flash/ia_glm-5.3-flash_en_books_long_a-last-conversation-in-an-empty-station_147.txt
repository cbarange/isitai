The last train had gone at eleven minutes past midnight, and after it, the station seemed to exhale. The announcement boards went dark one by one, their amber letters dissolving like sugar in water, and what remained was a cathedral of echoing tile and iron, lit by lamps that buzzed with the uneasiness of things left on when no one is watching.

They stood on platform three, where the paint on the yellow line had worn to a pale suggestion, and the wind that came through the tunnel smelled of rain that had fallen somewhere else, hours ago, in a country neither of them would visit.

"You could still make the six o'clock," she said, though they both knew the six o'clock was a fiction now, a habit of speech left over from years when their lives had run on timetables.

"I know," he said. "I'm not going to."

She laughed, briefly, the way one laughs at a funeral when grief misfires. Above them, the clock's minute hand shuddered and did not move. It had stopped, she realized, at 4:47 — some afternoon long past, some failure no one had ever been sent to repair. Time in this place had simply given up pretending.

He set his bag down on the bench, deliberately, the way a man sets down a weight he has decided to stop carrying. The leather had gone soft at the handles; she remembered when he'd bought it, in another city, in another weather. She remembered too much. That was the trouble with long acquaintance: every object in the world came annotated.

"Do you remember," he began, and she raised a hand.

"Don't. If we start remembering we'll be here until the cleaners come, and they'll sweep us into the bins with the tickets and the coffee cups."

But she was smiling, and he saw it, and the seeing made the silence between them change texture — from the silence of strangers waiting for different trains into the older silence, the one they had built together out of shared beds and shared winters and the particular way each of them said nothing when the other was sad.

Somewhere above, a pigeon moved along the girders, its claws ticking on the iron like a slow typewriter. The station had gone back to being what stations are when empty: not a place of departure but a place of suspension, where every journey is imagined and none is taken, where the tracks run out into blackness toward cities that glitter only in the mind.

"I used to think," she said at last, "that love was a train. That you either caught it or you didn't, and missing it was the tragedy."

"And now?"

"Now I think love is the platform. The trains come and go. What stays is the waiting, if you choose to keep waiting. Or the standing here, together, when there's nothing left to wait for."

He turned to look at her fully. The lamplight fell across half her face and left the other half to the dark, and he thought that this was the truest portrait of her he had ever seen: half chosen, half mystery, and he had loved both halves without ever learning to name the difference.

"We were good," he said. "For a long time, we were very good."

"We were," she agreed. "That's what makes it hard. If we'd been bad at it, we could hate each other, and hatred is a kind of company. But we were good, and so all we have is this tenderness, which has nowhere to live anymore."

The wind moved through the station again, lifting a newspaper page from the tiles and carrying it along the platform like a pale, frantic bird before pinning it against the shuttered kiosk. On the far wall, a mosaic of the region's rivers ran in faded blue from one end of the hall to the other, and she looked at it and thought: all those waters reaching the sea by different roads, and none of them regretting the bends.

He picked up his bag. Not to leave — there was nowhere, at this hour, for leaving to mean anything — but because holding it made his hands feel they had a purpose, and purpose was easier than the other thing, the raw thing, the thing they had come here to say and had circled all night like moths around a cold bulb.

"I'll write," he said, and then, hearing the falseness of it, corrected himself: "No. I won't write. I'll think of you at odd hours. That's more honest, if less useful."

She nodded. "I'll do the same. Odd hours are where I keep you, I think. You've always lived there."

They did not embrace. Some farewells are too exact for touching, and the distance of a meter between them was itself the final, formal act — the measured space that said: we agree on this, we agree on where it ends.

When the cleaners came at four, they found the platform empty, the benches wiped clean of dust by nothing, the clock still fixed at 4:47, and on the yellow line, side by side, two faint scuffs in the worn paint that might have been made by anyone, or by no one, standing a long time in one place before walking, separately, out into the early and indifferent light.
