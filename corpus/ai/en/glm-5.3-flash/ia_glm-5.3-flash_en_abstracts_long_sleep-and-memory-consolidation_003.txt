**Background.** Sleep is widely recognized as a critical state for the consolidation of newly acquired memories, yet the specific contributions of different sleep stages—rapid eye movement (REM) sleep and non-REM slow-wave sleep (SWS)—to distinct memory systems remain debated. According to the active systems consolidation hypothesis, memory traces initially dependent on the hippocampus are progressively redistributed toward neocortical networks for long-term storage, a process facilitated by the covert replay of neuronal firing patterns during sleep. However, most human evidence derives from correlational designs or from studies using sleep deprivation, which confounds memory consolidation with the non-specific effects of fatigue, elevated sleep pressure, and stress. Furthermore, the question of whether sleep selectively enhances hippocampus-dependent declarative memories (e.g., word pairs, spatial navigation) versus non-declarative skills (e.g., motor sequence learning) is unresolved, as is the relative contribution of sleep spindles, slow oscillations, and REM sleep to these processes. Clarifying these mechanisms has direct relevance for educational practice and for therapeutic interventions in populations with memory impairments, such as older adults and patients with sleep disorders. The present study was designed to dissociate the specific role of post-learning sleep from mere wakefulness, and to test whether the beneficial effect of sleep is selective for declarative memory or generalizes across memory systems.

**Methods.** We conducted a randomized, counterbalanced, within-subject crossover study involving 48 healthy young adults (24 women; mean age = 22.4 years, SD = 2.1) free of neurological, psychiatric, and sleep disorders, as verified by clinical interview and actigraphy. Participants learned two memory tasks in the evening: (i) a declarative task consisting of 40 word pairs and a virtual navigation task (2D arena learning), and (ii) a non-declarative task consisting of a finger-tapping motor sequence (5-digit sequence, 12 trials). Following encoding, participants were assigned to one of two conditions separated by one week: a full night of polysomnographically monitored sleep (Sleep condition) or a night of total sleep deprivation under controlled supervision (Wake condition), with retrieval testing performed the following morning at 10:00 in both conditions, prior to any recovery sleep. Polysomnographic recordings quantified time spent in SWS (N3), REM sleep, and spindle density (sigma power, 12–15 Hz) at central electrodes. Retrieval performance was measured as the percentage of correctly recalled word pairs, navigation latency and path efficiency, and speed gain on the motor sequence (number of correct sequences per 30-second block), with baseline-to-test change scores as the primary dependent variables. Data were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models with condition (Sleep vs. Wake), task, and their interaction as fixed effects and participant as a random effect; correlations between sleep parameters and memory change were assessed with Pearson coefficients corrected for multiple comparisons.

**Results.** Sleep-dependent improvement was observed for both memory domains, but with distinct magnitudes and electrophysiological correlates. For declarative memory, participants recalled significantly more word pairs after sleep than after wakefulness (mean retention 84.6% vs. 72.1%; β = 12.5 percentage points, 95% CI [7.9, 17.1], p < .001), and navigation path efficiency was better preserved following sleep (p = .004). For procedural memory, speed gains on the finger-tapping task were substantially larger after sleep (mean gain 21.8% vs. 4.3%; β = 17.5 percentage points, 95% CI [11.2, 23.8], p < .001), indicating that sleep-dependent consolidation is not restricted to declarative systems. The Condition × Task interaction was significant (p = .03), driven by the larger sleep-related benefit for motor skill speed relative to recall accuracy. Importantly, overnight change in declarative recall correlated positively with the amount of SWS (r = .41, p = .008) and with slow oscillation-spindle coupling strength (r = .38, p = .012), whereas motor sequence improvement correlated with spindle density (r = .44, p = .004) but not with REM duration (r = .11, p = .52). Total sleep time was unrelated to performance change after adjusting for SWS and spindle measures. No condition differences were found at baseline encoding, confirming equivalent initial learning (all p > .20), and subjective sleepiness ratings did not mediate the sleep effect when included as covariates.

**Conclusion.** These findings support the active systems consolidation framework by showing that a single night of post-learning sleep, compared with matched wakefulness, robustly enhances both declarative and non-declarative memory consolidation in young adults, with distinct electrophysiological signatures: slow-wave activity and spindle–slow oscillation coupling for hippocampus-dependent memories, and spindle density for procedural skills. The dissociable correlates across memory systems suggest stage- and oscillation-specific mechanisms rather than a global, unitary effect of sleep, and argue against fatigue or sleep pressure as sufficient explanations of the sleep benefit. Because performance after sleep deprivation remained stable rather than degraded for some measures, sleep appears to act primarily by actively promoting consolidation rather than merely protecting memories from interference. Future studies should extend these results to older adults and clinical populations, and test whether targeted memory reactivation or closed-loop stimulation of slow oscillations can enhance the identified spindle- and SWS-dependent mechanisms, with potential applications to learning optimization and cognitive rehabilitation.
